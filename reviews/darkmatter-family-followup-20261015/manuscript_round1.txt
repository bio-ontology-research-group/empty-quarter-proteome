              Known phyla, unknown proteome:
         a controlled census of the Rub’ al-Khali soil
                         metagenome
                                   A. Lopez Velazquez et al.
                        King Abdullah University of Science and Technology



Abstract
Reference coverage limits interpretation of microbial proteomes.          We analysed 551 soil
metagenomes from the Rub’ al-Khali and recovered 5,438 species-level genome representatives.
Among 43.4 million protein clusters linked to genome bins, 9.89% lacked a detected UniRef50
match; 106,042 recurrent families contained 12.5% of these dark representatives. Evolutionary
and culture comparisons exposed dependence on reading frame, population weighting and gene
calling. Darkness varied by lineage: 237 Thermoplasmatota representatives had a median dark
fraction of 31.0%, with within-lineage associations involving shared-protein coverage. Among
initially low-query-score dark models, cropping to confident regions recovered reference matches
for 74.07% of those unmatched as whole chains in a paired search. Equal-depth, length-matched
soil and global reference catalogues differed by 18.54 percentage points in dark-family recovery,
compared with 3.10 points for annotated controls. Our catalogue connects reference-absent
proteins to genomes and repeated sampling, with controls identifying how reference composition
and query definition shape unknown proteomes.


Introduction
Drylands cover roughly two fifths of the land surface and are projected to expand [1, 2], and their
hot hyperarid interiors are the low-biomass case in which contamination approaches the detection
limit [3]. The Rub’ al-Khali, the largest contiguous sand sea, had no genome-resolved microbial
survey. A companion 16S rRNA survey of the same transect mapped its bacterial ecology by
landscape, soil position and rainfall [4], and listed what amplicons cannot reach: gene content
without primer bias, function tied to a genome, and the archaea.
    What genomes reveal is mostly unknown. Across global catalogues a large share of microbial
protein families match no characterised family, and the size of that share depends on the refer-
ence and the rule used to call it [5–7]. The unknown families are numerous, span species, evolve
under purifying selection and concentrate in uncultivated lineages [8]; most are restricted to one
ecosystem [9]; and large structural surveys extend domain assignments beyond sequence-based
annotations [10, 11]. Interpreting the remaining reference-absent proteins requires separating
evidence for coding status from evidence for structural or functional novelty. Repeated envi-
ronmental sampling provides an opportunity to connect these questions to the persistence and
genomic context of individual families.
    A reference no-hit depends on gene prediction, database composition and search sensitivity.
Recurrent families can support evolutionary comparisons, while culture-derived assemblies offer
an independent reconstruction of DNA from related organisms. Known-fold proteins processed
through a structural pipeline provide a retrieval control. Combining these comparisons in one
sampling programme can delimit which parts of an unknown repertoire are supported by each
form of evidence.

    Here we combine these comparisons in one biome. We shotgun sequenced 551 soil samples
from 61 sampled locations across the Rub’ al-Khali on five expeditions over 31 months, at three
soil positions and with elemental chemistry, recovered 5,438 species-level genomes, and measured
the unknown fraction of their proteome against a control at every layer: annotated-family controls
for selection and reference absence; long-read genomes of strains cultured from soils of the same
basin; blinded known-fold controls for structure; and matched comparisons for a lineage with
a high dark fraction. The comparisons distinguish frame-dependent evolutionary estimates in
recurrent families from variation in reference coverage across lineages, genomes and structural
search criteria.


Results
Genome recovery and taxonomic coverage
We sampled 59 core transect sites and two auxiliary locations along a 1,043 km transect of the
Rub’ al-Khali on five expeditions between March 2023 and October 2025, at the soil surface,
in the subsurface and beside plant roots, and shotgun sequenced 551 soil samples to a median
depth of 21.58 Gbp per sample (Fig. 1a,b). Of the 549 samples that assembled, two SemiBin2
binnings (pretrained model and self-supervised) refined by DAS Tool yielded 14,426 bins, 4,225
near-complete and 8,209 additional medium-completeness bins under the operational CheckM2
thresholds; dereplication at 95% average nucleotide identity gave 5,438 species representatives,
including 2,259 near-complete genomes (Fig. 1c). Full MIMAG assessment classified 126 as high
quality and 5,312 as medium quality. Taxonomy throughout is GTDB release 232.
    Every representative was placed to class rank, within 51 GTDB phylum labels. The first empty
taxonomic rank was order for 11 representatives, family for 142, genus for 1,343 and species for
3,755; 187 carry a species assignment. These are mutually exclusive depths of the first empty
rank, not counts of validated new taxa. Of the 11 order-level gaps, the 10 with adequate marker
coverage were chimera-clean by GUNC (clade separation 0.01 to 0.32 against a 0.45 threshold),
and de novo trees with release-calibrated relative evolutionary divergence supported no new order:
six are single-genome lineages and the two multi-genome clades sit at genus and family depth.
Empty-rank depth is distinct from a naming heuristic that flags an empty or digit-bearing GTDB
label (Table 1). The latter is a proxy for reference naming coverage.

Recurrent-family comparisons depend on reading frame and gene calling
Gene prediction on the 552 assembly files gave 753,305,533 proteins, which collapsed to
449,265,306 cluster representatives at 90% identity over 80% of the length. A cluster was called
sequence-dark when none of its searched members returned a UniRef50 hit under a sensitive
search with an E-value threshold and no identity or coverage filter (Methods); 69,938,628 repre-
sentatives (15.6%) were dark. Restricting to clusters with at least one member on a contig of the
37,949 bins meeting the operational CheckM2 medium-or-better thresholds in the two binnings
before refinement gives the genome-resolved census: 4,292,446 of 43,423,322 representatives
(9.89%) have no reference homolog. Both percentages are shares of cluster representatives, not
of proteins or of species. Within the genome-resolved dark set, 65.8% of representatives are
shorter than 100 residues and 56.4% are partial gene calls. Across the assembly-wide catalogue,

all 449 million representatives, including dark clusters, 29.7% are shorter than 100 residues and
87.5% are partial. These distributions use different catalogue scopes.
    We selected recurrent families to examine sequence constraint and distribution. Clustering
the 4,292,446 genome-resolved dark representatives at 50% identity produced 3,658,797 clus-
ters: 3,353,521 singletons, 196,450 two-member clusters and 108,826 clusters with at least three
members. Requiring at least three samples and two sites retained 106,042 families with 536,437
members, or 12.5% of the dark representatives. The other 2,784 clusters with at least three
members failed the spread criterion. An additional 782,616 singleton or two-member clusters
recurred across the required samples and sites but remained outside the admitted family set. The
median admitted family was assembled in 12 samples at 9 sites. Subsequent family analyses are
conditional on this recurrence-based selection.
    With reads from all 551 samples mapped to the member genes, the median family is detected in
98 samples, four families are confined to one sample, and 79.4% of families are detected in all five
expeditions (94.8% in at least four). Under the stricter criterion of independent assembly, 28.9%
were assembled in all five. Detection by covered fraction favours short genes, and these frequencies
describe prevalence under the mapping rule. Five-expedition detection is 89.7% for families
whose representative is shorter than 60 residues and 67.1% for those of at least 250 residues;
a 150-base read can cover half a coding sequence of up to 300 bases, subject to the alignment
rule. An exploratory null that permuted expedition labels within site and soil position, holding
family occupancy and co-occurrence fixed, gave a mean all-five frequency of 82.7% (central 95%
null range 81.1–84.2%; 499 permutations). The observed frequency was 3.36 percentage points
lower (lower-tail permutation p = 0.002). A sensitivity that preserved whole visits within site,
position and replicate-count strata gave a null mean of 80.5% (95% range 78.9–82.1%; lower-tail
p = 0.11). The apparent deficit depends on the exchangeability unit. Repeated detection is
therefore interpreted relative to prevalence and sampling structure; expedition-associated assay
differences, mapping ambiguity and persistent DNA remain possible contributors.
    The median pairwise dN/dS across 97,200 evaluable dark families is 0.425, with 96.7% below
one, against 0.388 (99.6% below one, n = 95,647) for 106,042 UniRef50-matched families selected
using representative length and family size and passed through the same clustering, alignment and
estimation steps (Fig. 2a). The pooled distributions differ, and the groups also differ in sequence
divergence: median family mean dS is 0.607 in the dark arm and 0.863 in the control. Comparisons
therefore depend on divergence strata. For families with mean dS below 0.10, median dN/dS is
0.582 in the dark arm and 0.321 in the control; for mean dS between 0.5 and 1.0, the medians are
0.435 and 0.452. The control provides a reference distribution for the estimation pipeline, with
divergence and family-admission differences that remain relevant to interpretation.
    The difference between stratum medians was +0.261 (95% family-bootstrap interval 0.197–
0.288) at mean dS below 0.1 and −0.0163 (−0.0187 to −0.0142) at dS 0.5–1.0 (Fig. 2c). These
conditional intervals treat families as independent. Time dependence, population sampling and
correlated estimation error limit direct interpretation of pairwise dN/dS as selection strength
[12, 13].
    Coarsened matching on mean synonymous divergence, median coding-sequence length, GC
content and sequence count retained 61,427 families in each arm, with medians 0.386 for dark and
0.423 for annotated families. This subset retained 7.5% of dark families in the lowest divergence
stratum and 98.8% in the highest; 88.4% had mean dS at least 0.5. Using all families in the 553
common strata before equal-count sampling (93,220 dark and 95,541 annotated families), weighted
mean differences of family medians were +0.012 using the dark-arm distribution, −0.022 using
the annotated-arm distribution and −0.018 using overlap weights. These means and the pooled
medians are different estimands. The direction of the contrast depends on divergence and the
target population. Residual dS imbalance remained after matching (standardised mean difference
−0.124), and dS shares estimation noise with dN/dS.

spurious-family matches and overlaps among existing calls; detecting uncalled alternative ORFs
and establishing translation require additional sequence analysis and experimental evidence [16].
In particular, the overlap audit depends on both calls surviving Prodigal’s gene-selection step,
which suppresses heavily overlapping alternatives [17].

Per-genome darkness varies strongly among lineages
Per genome, the dark fraction is the share of protein instances on a representative’s contigs
whose cluster has no UniRef50 homolog; across the 5,438 representatives its median is 6.3%
(Fig. 3a) (interquartile range 3.7–10.4%), a per-genome rate. Assembly quality (completeness,
contamination, N50 and length together) explains 11.5% of the variance in that fraction. Adding
phylum raises the explained share to 76.8% and genus to 83.5%; 72.0% of the variance is unique
to lineage and 2.0% unique to quality. Larger genomes are slightly less dark (−0.096 per tenfold
increase in corrected size, R2 0.067), so corrected genome size accounts for a small share of the
variation. Taxonomic novelty predicts part of it: the median dark fraction falls from 0.217 for
genomes first flagged at phylum to 0.017 for genomes with no flagged rank (Fig. 3b), and within
Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota genomes with a placeholder genus are darker
than those with a named genus (medians 0.075 against 0.022, 0.051 against 0.030, 0.068 against
0.053). That much is expected, because a lineage absent from UniRef50 is dark by construction.
The lineage term persists after adjustment for novelty: with first empty or digit-bearing rank
in the model, phylum still adds 0.340 to R2 , 52% of its unconditioned increment. Phylum and
genus enter with far more degrees of freedom than the four quality covariates and the model treats
genomes as independent draws, so these increments are descriptive upper bounds; the like-for-like
test is the matched contrast in the next section.
    The residual is concentrated. After the quality model, Thermoplasmatota genomes are 0.21
darker than predicted, Cyanobacteriota 0.14 and Bdellovibrionota 0.10, while Halobacteriota,
Bacillota, Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota sit below the line. Thermoplasmatota have
the largest positive residual among these phyla; Actinomycetota contribute more dark protein
instances in absolute terms.

Thermoplasmatota carry a large share of the attributable dark families
The catalogue holds 237 Thermoplasmatota species: 185 in class SW-10-69-26, 51 in class E2
and one in Thermoplasmata, none with a named genus. GTDB release 232 holds 21 and 197
species representatives in the two classes, fewer together than this one sand sea. Their median
dark fraction is 0.310, against 0.046 for the other 354 archaea and 0.062 for the 4,847 bacteria,
and 236 of 237 lie above the bacterial 95th percentile.
    The obvious alternative is that rare, poorly assembled genomes look unknown. We there-
fore matched each Thermoplasmatota representative 1:1 without replacement to a comparator on

completeness, contamination, corrected genome size, N50, mean relative abundance and preva-
lence. Against other archaea, 218 of 237 found a match within one standardised unit; the matched
pairs have median dark fractions of 0.308 and 0.048, a 6.5-fold difference, with the Thermoplas-
matota genome the darker in 216 of 218 pairs. Against bacteria all 237 matched within half a
unit, with residual imbalance below 0.05 standardised units on every covariate; medians 0.310
against 0.069, 4.5-fold, darker in 237 of 237 pairs (Fig. 3c). The contrast persists after this quality
and abundance matching. In a separate comparison restricted to genomes whose first empty or
digit-bearing label occurs at class rank, the 236 Thermoplasmatota representatives have median
darkness 0.310, compared with 0.125 in 324 other representatives, a 2.5-fold contrast. These
descriptive comparisons share phylogenetic dependence within lineages.
    To examine reference proximity within Thermoplasmatota, we fitted a phylogenetic gener-
alised least-squares model across the 237 representatives using amino-acid identity to an external
reference and the fraction of proteins with reciprocal best hits, together with assembly-quality
covariates. Darkness decreased with shared-protein coverage (coefficient −0.1375, standard er-
ror 0.0195) and increased conditionally with amino-acid identity (0.00170 per percentage point,
standard error 0.00041); both directions persisted in six sensitivity specifications (Supplementary
Note S1). These associations describe complementary aspects of reference representation within
the sampled lineage.
    Thermoplasmatota contribute a small share of community abundance and a larger share of the
attributable unknown repertoire. They are 4.4% of species and 0.8% of mapped reads (median
across samples), yet carry 19.8% of the 77,069 single-phylum dark families and 15.2% of dark
protein instances, an enrichment of 4.6-fold by families and 4.0-fold by proteins; Actinomycetota,
half of the mapped community, carry dark families in proportion to their species share (1.1-fold).
    These are not rare organisms. Thermoplasmatota representatives are detected in 481 of 549
samples, a median of 21 species per sample, at a median 0.39% of reads, and they are the
cosmopolitan archaea of the sand sea where Halobacteriota are patchy (81 of 549 samples, in
bulk where present). Depth is their clearest soil-position association: across 45 sites with both
depths, the median site-level share is 0.72% of reads below the surface and 0.27% at the sur-
face, with the subsurface share higher at 40 sites (Wilcoxon p = 4 × 10−7 ). After division by
the mapped fraction, the subsurface share is higher at 38 of 45 sites (p = 8 × 10−7 ). The
root-adjacent contrast has mixed directions across sites (26 of 53, p = 0.66). Annotations in the
remaining approximately 70% provide a provisional metabolic profile. Across all 237 Thermoplas-
matota representatives, the annotation rules identify glycolysis in 82%, a glyoxylate-cycle module
in 79%, 2-oxoglutarate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase markers in 96%, both cytochrome c oxidase
subunits in 59% and archaeal ATPase markers in 91%. Branched-chain amino-acid transport
markers occur in 79% and sugar transport markers in 2%; serine-protease, endoglucanase and
chitinase annotations occur in 96%, 79% and 44%, respectively. Archaellum-associated markers
occur in 236 of 237 representatives (99.6%) and CRISPR-associated markers in 79%. These per-
centages describe the full lineage set. In the separate comparison of 181 completeness-matched
Thermoplasmatota–Halobacteriota pairs, glyoxylate-module calls occur in 139 versus 63 genomes
(76.8% versus 34.8%), branched-chain amino-acid carbon-skeleton markers in three versus 165
(1.7% versus 91.2%), and catalase markers in 49 versus 153 (27.1% versus 84.5%). Sugar trans-
port markers occur in four versus 132 (2.2% versus 72.9%). The full 237-genome set includes
no detected marker annotations for methanogenesis, ammonia oxidation or rhodopsin; rhodopsin
markers occur in 51 of all 269 Halobacteriota. These annotations motivate tests of peptide and
polysaccharide use in subsurface archaea. Physiological pathway completeness, auxotrophy and
rhodopsin absence require validation with lineage-sensitive models and experimental measure-
ments. Approximately a third of the Thermoplasmatota proteins remain without a UniRef50
match.

Confident regions recover structural reference matches
Sequence search misses remote homology, so the 1,466,943 genome-resolved dark representatives
of at least 100 residues were screened by predicted structure (Methods). A 3Di search against
the AlphaFold Database (v6, 241,070,489 entries) and the PDB (release 250101) left 997,646
(68.0%) with no hit in either, forming the candidates for coordinate-based validation. Structural
clustering of those candidates and admission at three or more members gave 17,221 fold families
with one model each, 13,770 folded here with ESMFold and 3,451 reused from an earlier run.
Only 2,838 models (16.5%) passed the confidence gate (mean pLDDT ≥ 0.70, ≥ 100 residues);
the gate is on confidence.
    Co-searched with them under the same command were 3,684 blinded controls: complete chains
and deterministic fragments of experimentally solved PDB chains, matched to the candidates’
length, composition and partiality and renamed before folding, of which 3,013 passed the same
gate. After gating, mean pLDDT was 0.775 in dark models and 0.867 in controls, so the retained
confidence distributions differ. Against both references, 1,031 of 2,838 dark models (36.3%) fell
below a query-normalised TM-score of 0.5, compared with 101 of 3,013 controls (3.35%; Fig. 4a).
Raw-hit inspection separates retrieval from scoring: 22 dark models and 66 controls returned no
rows from either reference. Among models with a retrieved alignment, 1,009 of 2,816 dark models
and 35 of 2,947 controls fell below the query threshold. This conditional comparison describes
retrieved alignments; the primary denominator retains all qualified models and includes retrieval
failures.
    We tested whether confident regions within whole-chain-unmatched models recover structural
matches by searching the complete chains and their confidence-defined crops against a frozen
PDB reference with the same query-normalised threshold. Across all 5,851 qualified dark and
control models, this procedure extracted 7,773 regions; 161 models had no eligible region. Of
the 1,031 dark models below the initial dual-reference query threshold, 964 remained unmatched
as whole chains in this PDB search. Cropping recovered a match for 714 of these 964 models
(74.07%); 775 of all 1,031 had at least one matched crop, including models already matched as
whole chains (Fig. 4b,c). With the source PDB entry excluded, 2,889 of 3,013 controls had a
matched crop (95.88%). A GPU ungapped-prefilter sensitivity recovered 731 of 961 whole-chain-
unmatched models in the same initial dark group (Supplementary Note S1). These paired results
show that structural reference coverage increases when the query is a confident region. Cropping
jointly changes sequence extent, score normalisation and search opportunity.
    Pfam recognises a domain in 8.75% of the 4,880 member proteins of the 1,031 families and in
14.0% of those families, compared with 90.7% of the controls. Restricting the dark comparison
to one representative per family, Pfam hits occur in 93/1,031 models below the query-normalised
threshold (9.02%) and 101/1,807 above it (5.59%). Low Pfam coverage therefore occurs in both
sequence-dark subsets. Sequence and structural searches therefore provide different descriptions
of reference coverage. The controls derive from experimentally solved PDB chains and calibrate
recovery under the stated source-entry and sequence-identity exclusions. Experimental structures
and curated domain boundaries would further resolve the remaining unmatched regions.
    We also asked whether the two structural groups differed in their genomic neighbourhoods.
Among 353 families with complete, coordinate-verified flanks in at least two species, a flank-
ing KEGG orthologue recurred across species in 40.46% of the 173 low-query-score families and
32.22% of the 180 reference-matched sequence-dark families, compared with same-contig posi-
tional expectations of 8.71% and 6.56%, respectively. The covariate-adjusted difference in excess
recurrence was 3.54 percentage points (stratified permutation p = 0.407); this comparison is
conditional on the 24.16% of multispecies families that passed the complete-flank gate (Supple-
mentary Note S1).

Reference composition affects recovery of dark families
Two ceilings measure how far existing references reach into this proteome. The first is annota-
tion: of the 19,186,853 protein-coding genes in the 5,438 representatives, 55.6% carry no KEGG
orthologue, and the value is 53.9% in the near-complete representatives alone, showing that in-
complete annotation persists in this subset. The second is prediction. In the 448 samples with
both a genome profile and a 16S profile from the companion survey, PICRUSt2 emits all 10,543
orthologues in its reference set for 419 samples (93.5%) at the presence threshold of abundance
greater than zero. Under this nearly saturated prediction rule, median recall of genome-encoded
orthologues is 86.0% and median Jaccard similarity is 0.634. A median of 3,077.5 predicted ortho-
logues per sample are absent from that sample’s recovered genomes; incomplete genome recovery
limits their interpretation. Spearman ρ on shared orthologues is 0.839 (median weighted nearest-
taxon index 0.159; Fig. 5a). Across the catalogue, 2,517 of the 11,374 encoded orthologues (22%)
lie outside the predictor’s emitted set. In 420 of 448 samples, every genome-encoded orthologue
inside that set is predicted, so presence-recall in those samples measures overlap with the ref-
erence universe. An exploratory fixed-universe rank comparison found a small matching-sample
advantage after conditioning on expedition, soil position and site (mean correlation difference
0.0023 across 146 exchangeable samples; Supplementary Note S2).
     We next tested whether sequence-dark families have detectable homologues in additional cat-
alogues. We searched the 106,042 dark family representatives together with the 106,042 matched
UniRef50-matched families against MGnify90, GEM, SPIRE and five desert catalogues, 1.258
billion sequences, in one DIAMOND pass: 28.74% of dark families (30,479) had no hit in any
of them, compared with 0.41% of the control (435) (Fig. 5b). Absence here is defined by the
catalogue and the search rule, not by geography; MGnify90 biome labels carry no coordinates.
What the control isolates is which reference is missing. The five desert catalogues together hold
147.5 million sequences, a fifth of MGnify90, yet leave 42.60% of dark families unexplained against
MGnify90’s 52.68%. Compared with an analytical expectation for random retention of MGnify90
sequences at the same size (a rarefaction model whose full-catalogue endpoint agrees with the
observed MGnify90 absence to 0.15 percentage points), the desert catalogues leave 24.33 fewer
points of dark families unexplained and SPIRE 21.30 fewer, while the same comparison for known
proteins moves by about one point (Fig. 5b). The larger-release sensitivity search recovered a
small additional fraction. Against the complete MGnify90 2026 07 release (1.66 billion sequences,
searched with a different aligner, so a robustness check), 5.8% of the absent dark families gain
a hit while 34.3% of the control’s residual absences are resolved, leaving 27.08% of dark families
unmatched. The absent families are not rare or local within the basin: their median occupancy is
37 of 61 sampled locations against 38 for families with a hit, and the medians range from 44 versus
50 locations in the shortest stratum to the values shown in Fig. 5c. The combined desert catalogue
and SPIRE recover more dark families than the analytical MGnify90 expectation. Their redun-
dancy, assembly basis and biome composition differ, so the comparison combines these properties.
The matched-reference comparison below tests the effect after controlling sequence depth, length
and redundancy.
     To test reference composition at equal sequence depth, we compared proteins from a fixed ran-
dom sample of 1,500 soil MAGs from SMAG [18] with 2,055 GEM genomes. Both arms used the
same gene caller, sequence deduplication and exact one-residue length quotas, leaving 4,210,592
proteins and 1,295,970,055 residues in each reference. Among the 106,042 dark queries, 28.35%
had a SMAG hit and 9.81% a GEM hit, a paired difference of 18.54 percentage points; the cor-
responding control difference was 3.10 points (93.99% versus 90.89%). The dark-query difference
ranged from 16.83 to 18.54 points across three GEM draws, remained positive in every length
stratum, and persisted after removing sequences shared between reference arms and imposing
30% sequence identity (Supplementary Note S1). Reference composition therefore affects recov-
ery beyond these matched properties; the remaining contrast includes taxonomic representation,
genome recovery and within-catalogue diversity.

To examine the biological value of the catalogue within a recognizable protein family, we anal-

extensions. After exact sequence deduplication, Saccharimonadia contributed 22 EQ, ten GEM

Discussion
This catalogue links reference-absent protein clusters to genomes and repeated sampling of a hy-
perarid environment. Evidence is strongest for the recurrent subset: 106,042 families hold 12.5%
of genome-resolved dark representatives. Most evaluable families have median pairwise dN/dS
below one, and the annotated frame usually has a lower estimate than the shifted frame. The
annotated-frame contrast is evidence about reading-frame-dependent sequence patterns. Because
most artifact-flagged families also have dN/dS below one, the estimate alone cannot establish
coding status. Recurrence and recruitment describe the persistence of homologous DNA; expres-
sion measurements and overlap-aware gene validation would further resolve coding status and
biological activity.
    Darkness also varies strongly among lineages. Quality- and abundance-matched comparisons
retain a large Thermoplasmatota contrast, while a comparison at the same placeholder rank gives
a smaller 2.5-fold difference. Reference proximity and shared ancestry therefore remain relevant
to the lineage pattern. Maeke et al. [21] examined the relationship between rarity and unknown-
gene content in other Thermoplasmatota. Within the desert lineage, the phylogenetic model
associates darkness with lower shared-protein coverage after conditioning on amino-acid identity
and assembly quality. The positive conditional identity coefficient highlights the distinction be-
tween similarity among shared genes and the extent of shared gene content. Additional close
references would test the stability of these associations across reference sampling.


                                                9
    The culture comparison directly exposes gene-call dependence: 343 protein-unmatched dark
calls have nucleotide support in the culture assembly but no Bakta CDS covering half the aligned
region. Including nucleotide support narrows the dark-versus-annotated sharing contrast. Gene
prediction and annotation filtering therefore make a substantial contribution to the protein-level
gap in these bacterial pairs; the residual gap remains sensitive to strain divergence, assembly
and alignment thresholds. The controlled caller substitution reduces the broader call-set dis-
crepancy while leaving the small admitted-family subset unchanged in aggregate. Full codon
correspondence and evidence of translation would help resolve the remaining cases. The current
control evidence also has limited campaign coverage: one no-template library was sequenced with
expedition four, and the catalogue-level lineage and environmental summaries still include the
flagged representatives. Sensitivity analyses across these summaries are required to assess their
contribution.
    The global family catalogues established that unknown families are numerous, span species,
evolve under purifying selection and concentrate in uncultivated taxa [5, 6, 8], that restriction to
one ecosystem is the norm (63.4% of 608,258 families; [9]), and that structural surveys recover
many familiar folds and domain combinations [10, 11]. Our 28.74% reference absence is unremark-
able as a fraction, and 36.3% of confident models fall below the whole-query similarity threshold,
and matches emerge for most initially unmatched models after cropping to confident regions. The
paired searches and source-excluded controls establish the dependence of reference coverage on
query extent. Genomic neighbourhood recurrence supplies additional context for both structural
groups; the adjusted comparison remains inconclusive about a between-group difference. Curated
domain boundaries and experimental structures would refine the interpretation of the remain-
ing regions. This study links families to repeated sampling within one biome and measures a
reference-composition effect: the desert-derived comparison explains 24 percentage points more
of the dark families than the analytical E-value-corrected expectation at the same size, against
about one point for the annotated control. SPIRE gives a 21.30-point gain and GEM a 4.27-point
gain, while Atacama has 4.67 points more absence than expected. The matched SMAG–GEM
comparison retains a larger gain for dark queries after equalising gene calling, exact sequence
redundancy, protein count and length distribution. Taxonomic and genome-recovery differences
remain part of this reference-composition contrast.
    The heliorhodopsin case study gives a biological example of additional sequence coverage
within a recognizable family. Its contribution lies in divergent candidates linked to genomes
and supported by their original reads. The published-panel comparison and sequence-derived
structural predictions constrain their interpretation; biochemical measurements are needed to
establish what these candidates do.
    Supplementary functional profiles provide exploratory context for the genome resource. The
companion amplicon comparison gives a median rank correlation of 0.839 on shared orthologues,
alongside a reference-coverage limit: 55.6% of protein-coding genes have no KEGG orthologue
assignment.
    These measurements describe predicted gene content and DNA-based abundance. Incomplete
genomes reduce sensitivity to gene presence, with consequences that depend on how genome
quality varies among the compared communities; half the representatives are below 90% complete.
The frame-shifted null calibrates whether the annotated frame is the selected one, not neutral
evolution in general. What any dark family does is not measured here.
    The two archaeal classes are culture and fold targets; all 22 retained representative–culture
groups are bacterial. Strain-level persistence across five expeditions is the next study on these
reads.

Methods
Sampling, sequencing and environmental data
Soil was sampled on five expeditions along a west to east transect through the Saudi Rub’ al-
Khali from the western escarpment (19.15◦ N, 45.17◦ E) to the Omani border (20.71◦ N, 54.73◦ E), a
cumulative path of 1,043 km with 60 numbered sites at predetermined coordinates and ten named
locations. The expeditions ran on 17–20 March 2023, 14 July 2023 (restricted by heat to eight
sites), 17–21 February 2024, 19–21 August 2024 and 12–15 October 2025. At each site and visit
three soil positions were sampled in three co-located replicates: surface soil at 0 cm, subsurface
soil at 5–10 cm, and soil from the root zone at 5–10 cm, termed root-adjacent because soil was
not consistently attached to roots. Soil was held at −20◦ C in the field and at −80◦ C in the
laboratory. Sampling design and field handling are shared with the companion 16S rRNA survey
and field-data descriptor [4, 22]. The 551 soil samples with complete metadata (117 surface, 166
subsurface, 268 root-adjacent) are the cohort used throughout; they cover 59 of the 60 transect
sites and two named locations, the 61 sampled locations used in occupancy statistics. Narrower
cohorts are defined where used.
    DNA was extracted from 0.9–1.2 g of soil with the QIAGEN PowerLyzer PowerSoil kit (12855-
50) or the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (47014), homogenised on a PowerLyzer 24, eluted in 50 µL
and quantified with the Qubit high-sensitivity double-stranded DNA assay. DNA was fragmented
on a Covaris instrument to 350 bp, purified with AMPure XP beads at 1.6:1, prepared with the
QIAseq FX and NEBNext Ultra II kits and sequenced at KAUST in 150-nt paired-end mode on
Illumina NovaSeq 6000 and NovaSeq X instruments. The submission comprises 554 runs for 551
samples: 221 NovaSeq 6000 runs and 333 NovaSeq X runs across four sequencing batches. The
sample-to-run manifest records instrument, library kit and batch; three samples have two sub-
mitted runs. The exact commands combining those runs remain unverified, and sequencing-batch
sensitivity remains unresolved for the exploratory environmental analyses. Reads were processed
with fastp v1.0.1 [23] with base correction in overlapping pairs, a qualified-base threshold of Q20,
at most 40% unqualified bases per read, a minimum length of 50 nt and a low-complexity filter
at 20%; surviving pairs and orphaned reads were both retained. The median sample retained
21.58 Gbp (156.6 million reads; IQR 16.53–25.08 Gbp).
    Soil chemistry and climate covariates are those of the companion survey restricted to the
samples profiled here: X-ray fluorescence total elemental contents of archived soil (248 profiled
samples with chemistry; 251 in the family-level cohort) and reanalysis climate series (453 and
456 samples). The retained laboratory XRF table is distinct from the fifth-expedition handheld
field measurements. Units, instrument metadata and detection-limit coding require confirmation.
Climate inputs were retrieved from the Open-Meteo archive as daily temperature and precipita-
tion series over twelve-month windows centred on each visit; retained tables record the start and
end dates. The provider’s model selection and original API responses were not archived. The
de Martonne index divides annualised precipitation by mean temperature plus ten. The environ-
mental analysis did not include pH. The complete analysis table rebuilds byte-for-byte from the
preserved metadata, laboratory and climate summary tables; regenerating the original climate
retrieval is a separate provenance gap.

Assembly, binning, dereplication and taxonomy
Each sample was assembled with MEGAHIT v1.2.9 [24] (meta-large preset, contigs ≥ 500 nt,
k-mers seen at least twice, pruning level 1, low-local-coverage ratio 0.3), supplying pairs and or-
phaned reads; 549 of the 551 samples produced an assembly (51Sr1 produced no contigs and F5Dr1
an empty file). Assembly statistics used QUAST v5.2.0 [25]. Every assembly was binned twice
with SemiBin2 v2.2.1 [26]: with the pretrained global model (single easy bin --environment
global --sequencing-type short read) on minimap2 [27] coverage of the paired reads, and
with a self-supervised per-sample model (single easy bin --self-supervised -m 1000) on


                                                11
BWA-MEM [28] coverage of pairs and orphans. They produced 16,333 and 21,616 bins of at
least medium quality, 37,949 in the union of the two unrefined binnings. The two bin sets were
reconciled per sample with DAS Tool v1.1.7 [29] at its defaults (score threshold 0.5, DIAMOND
search); of 542 samples binned by both runs, 539 yielded refined bins, 14,426 in total. Quality
was estimated with CheckM2 v1.1.0 [30] and tiered by completeness and contamination alone:
near-complete at completeness ≥ 90% and contamination ≤ 5% (4,225), medium at ≥ 50% and
≤ 10% inclusive (8,209; two bins sit at exactly 10.0%), the rest below these thresholds (1,992).
These operational tiers use CheckM2 alone. Subsequent barrnap v0.9 and tRNAscan-SE v2.0.12
[31] assessment of representatives required non-partial rRNA hits of at least 80, 900 and 1,900
nucleotides for 5S, 16S and 23S, respectively. This assessment found all three rRNAs in 157
genomes and at least 18 tRNA amino-acid types in 3,629. Full MIMAG classification gave 126
high-quality and 5,312 medium-quality representatives [32]. No chimerism screen was run on the
14,426 bins; GUNC v1.1.1 [33] against ProGenomes 3 was run on the ten order-gap candidates
only.
    The 12,434 bins of at least medium quality were dereplicated with dRep v3.6.2 [34] (fastANI
[35], primary and secondary ANI 0.90 and 0.95, completeness ≥ 50%, contamination ≤ 10%,
other parameters default; one bin was dropped internally), giving 5,438 species representatives
(2,259 near-complete, 3,179 additional genomes meeting the medium-completeness thresholds).
Representatives were classified with GTDB-Tk v2.7.2 [36] (classify wf, ANI pre-screen on)
against GTDB release 232 [37]; 1,898 classifications changed relative to an earlier run against
release 226, and every reported taxon is from release 232. The first-empty-rank census assigns
each representative to the first of the seven ranks from domain to species carrying an empty label;
classes are mutually exclusive. The ten order-gap candidates with adequate marker coverage were
placed on de novo trees (GTDB-Tk de novo wf) and assessed by relative evolutionary divergence
against release-232 rank intervals; the eleventh candidate (46% of marker positions aligned) was
excluded from adjudication.

Protein catalogue and the sequence-dark call
Genes were called on assemblies rather than on bins, so that the catalogue covers unbinned se-
quence; the 552 assembly files present at that step are a superset of the 549 canonical assemblies,
a catalogue-level basis that enters no per-sample cohort. Prediction used Pyrodigal v3.7.1 [17, 38]
(-p meta --no-stop-codon, partial genes retained): 753,305,533 proteins, clustered with MM-
seqs2 v18.8cc5c [39, 40] (linclust --min-seq-id 0.9 -c 0.8 --cov-mode 0) into 449,265,306
representatives, the unit counted in every dark rate. Every representative was searched against
UniRef50 release 2026 01 [41] with mmseqs search -s 7.5 -e 1e-3 --max-seqs 10, a pure E-
value criterion with no identity or coverage filter; a cluster is sequence-dark when no searched
member returned a hit. A cluster is genome-resolved when any member lies on a contig assigned
to one of the 37,949 unrefined bins of at least medium quality, tested over every member; this
membership rule, not read mapping, defines the 43,423,322 genome-resolved representatives and
the 4,292,446 dark ones among them. Functional annotation of the 5,438 representatives used
Bakta v1.12.0 [42] for gene calling and eggNOG-mapper v2.1.13 [43] with DIAMOND v2.0.15
[44] against the v2.1.12 reference data for KEGG orthologues. Representatives that refinement
emitted as strict contig subsets of a parent bin were annotated by restricting the parent’s gene
calls to their own contigs; the resulting table (rep ko v2) has no empty rows.

Read recruitment and detection
Two recruitment runs underlie the paper. The representative-level run mapped each sam-
ple’s paired reads to the 5,438 representatives with CoverM v0.7.0 [45] in genome mode (-p
minimap2-sr -m relative abundance, all other parameters default). No explicit read-identity
or aligned-read-fraction thresholds were set for this genome-level run. The default minimum cov-
ered fraction of 10% defines detection: a representative is detected when its relative abundance

exceeds zero, that is, when at least a tenth of its genome is covered. This is an inherited de-
fault, not a chosen threshold. The census and stress analyses use the frozen 548-sample profile
cohort available when those analyses were run; subsequent profiles are outside that cohort. The
later Thermoplasmatota ecology analysis used a separate 550-column snapshot: F13PRr1 was
absent and F5Dr1 contained no usable abundance values, leaving 549 samples. Consequently
its surface/subsurface comparison has 45 paired sites, whereas the earlier stress analysis has
44. These are analysis-specific frozen cohorts. Community values use two denominators: the
share of all reads (organisms outside the catalogue counted as non-carriers, a lower bound) and
the share of reads mapping to any representative (median unmapped fraction near 51%). The
member-aware run mapped the reads of all 551 samples to the 536,437 dark-family member
genes in contig mode (-m tpm covered fraction). A family is present when any member gene
reaches a covered fraction of 0.5 and its abundance is the summed member TPM. That rule is
easier to satisfy for a short gene and reverses the length to occupancy relation when replaced
by any-read presence, so occupancy is compared within length strata. The two shotgun control
libraries sequenced with the fourth expedition, a no-template extraction control (335,190 read
pairs after filtering) and a positive control of Hafnia paralvei (119,683,914 pairs; GTDB-Tk R232
on its assembly, ANI 98.99%), were profiled with the identical CoverM invocation and index.
Representatives detected in the no-template control are annotated as reagent contaminants in
the catalogue table and excluded from the pathogen census, which is therefore not reported;
the no-template assembly (11 contigs, 11 kb) was identified by UniRef50 search of its predicted
proteins. The read-profile signals identify Escherichia coli (V42PRr2 single SemiBin 33) and
Cutibacterium acnes (51Dr3 single SemiBin 390). A separate contig-alignment screen flags ten
additional representatives, including the Mixta alhagi representative T5PRr1 self SemiBin 614;
a vector-matching segment flags a possible contaminant contribution rather than establishing that
the entire bin is reagent-derived. Across these twelve representatives, the lineage-analysis table
contains 42,831 Pyrodigal protein instances, including 1,792 dark instances, out of 20,379,930 and
1,600,067, respectively. These instance denominators differ from the genome-linked cluster and
Bakta orthologue denominators. Excluding the twelve changes the catalogue median per-genome
dark fraction from 6.31% to 6.34%; abundance-weighted environmental effects require a separate
sensitivity analysis.

Dark families, matched control and selection
The 4,292,446 genome-resolved dark representatives were clustered with MMseqs2 easy-cluster
(--min-seq-id 0.5 -c 0.8 --cov-mode 0, default sensitivity 4) and each cluster mapped
through the 90%-identity membership table to the samples and sites in which its instances
were assembled. A cluster was admitted as a dark family when it held at least three distinct
90%-identity representatives assembled in at least three samples and two sites (106,042 families,
536,437 member genes; 2,784 three-member clusters failing the spread gate were dropped because
they failed the spread criterion). Member nucleotide sequences were excised from the assemblies
using the prediction coordinates. The control pool comprised every non-dark representative
on a contig of the same 37,949 bins, with darkness excluded against the assembly-wide dark
set. Because dark representatives are short, the pool was sampled per 10-residue length bin to
reproduce the dark length distribution (seed 20260813; the sample ran 12.5% short of target,
in the 20–39 residue bins), clustered identically, and control families were drawn to match dark
families by size bin (3–9 exactly, 10–19, 20 or more), one per dark family. Two asymmetries are
disclosed: control families were admitted on member count alone, without the sample and site
gate, and after admission the dark query set is longer than the control (median 105 against 75
residues). These differences, together with the divergent dS distributions, affect comparability;
their direction and size depend on the endpoint. Family admission uses the unrefined bins;
phylum attribution traces each member through its contig to the refined bin, the dereplicated
representative and its release-232 classification, which attributes 78,727 families.

Lineage darkness and the matched null
For each representative, every protein on its contigs was traced to its 90%-identity cluster and
scored dark or not by the cluster call above; the dark fraction is the share of dark protein instances,
computed on the refined winner contigs of all 5,438 representatives (native key, no renaming).
Ordinary least squares (statsmodels v0.14.2) modelled the dark fraction on assembly quality (com-
pleteness, contamination, log10 N50, log10 length), then adding phylum and genus (genera with
fewer than five representatives pooled; unnamed genera one level); unique and shared variance are
differences of R2 between nested models. Placeholder-name depth was coded as the first rank with
an empty or digit-bearing label, separately from the taxonomy census of empty ranks and added
to the quality model before phylum. No phylogenetic mixed model was fitted, so the representa-
tives enter as independent draws. Genome size was completeness-corrected length. The archaeal
contrast is a 1:1 nearest-neighbour match without replacement on standardised completeness,
contamination, log10 corrected size, log10 N50, log10 mean relative abundance and prevalence
(samples detected), against two pools reported separately, other archaea and bacteria, at calipers
of 0.5 to 2.0 standardised units (0.25 also against bacteria); residual imbalance is reported as
standardised mean differences. Dark-family attribution by phylum uses the 78,727 attributable

Supplementary Note S3: exploratory functional and environmen-

These analyses provide descriptive context for the catalogue. Laboratory XRF units, detection

Encoded function shifts with soil position, mostly by lineage turnover
DNA-damage repair is near-universal (base and nucleotide excision repair in 99.6% and 99.5% of
species) and compatible-solute synthesis is encoded by 79.5%. Biosynthetic gene clusters occur
in 97.5% of species but 85.3% of the 44,002 regions abut a contig edge, and acquired resistance
determinants occur in 37.4%, led by β-lactam (24.6%) and arsenic (17.3%) determinants, the
latter rising with calcium and falling with precipitation after conditioning on geography.
    Encoded-function contrasts were evaluated by soil position. For 282 KEGG modules and
4,127 orthologues we compared the share of the mapped community encoding each feature be-
tween paired samples of one site visit, root-adjacent soil against bulk soil at 52 sites and surface
against subsurface at 37, and kept a feature only if it cleared a 5-percentage-point floor at 5%
false-discovery rate and kept its sign when recomputed within Actinomycetota alone and within
Pseudomonadota alone. Fourteen modules pass beside roots. The root-adjacent community en-
codes more cytochrome bd oxidase (+13.0 points; +17.0 within Actinomycetota, +1.9 within
Pseudomonadota), serine-to-cysteine synthesis (+11.0), the Entner-Doudoroff pathway (+9.4),

at least 90% complete. These patterns describe differences in encoded capacity and community
composition.

Exploratory environmental associations
We explored associations between environmental covariates and the distribution of dark fami-
lies. Environmental covariates were associated with repertoire composition under the fitted mod-
els. Across the 547 samples with environmental annotation, composition of the 106,042-family

repertoire (105,977 families present in at least five samples) is structured by soil position (PER-
MANOVA R2 = 0.044) and by geographic position (0.079), and communities decay with distance
(Mantel r = 0.341). In the 251 samples with X-ray fluorescence chemistry, eleven geochemical
axes explain 13.2% of compositional variance, of which 8.9% survives conditioning on latitude and
longitude and 9.2% conditioning on position as well; climate (temperature, precipitation, aridity
index, n = 456) explains 3.0% beyond space. Partitioning the three blocks on the chemistry
cohort gives geochemistry 0.106 of unique variance against 0.055 for space and 0.057 for posi-

permutations (within-site restricted p = 0.884). Each R2 is a share of compositional variance
across families, not of any one family’s abundance.
    At family resolution, 94,568 families were testable against the chemistry axes and 100,293
against aridity. Sulfur associates with 54,227 families at 5% false-discovery rate, calcium with
47,174, chloride with 46,972, iron with 24,110 and the aridity index with 23,031; in each case

depends on compositionality and non-detect handling. Vanadium, molybdenum, nickel, cobalt,

this model, although the index remains within the hyperarid class along the transect.

the root-adjacent contrast to the 547 samples with recorded soil positions gives 101,897 testable
families, of which 20,136 are more abundant beside roots and 41,046 less abundant at 5% FDR.
On the same sample cohort, 94.1% of marginal associations retain significance after conditioning
on latitude and longitude, compared with 71 to 86% for the chemical axes. This retention statis-
tic describes sensitivity to those two covariates. Actinomycetota account for 52.7% of phylum-
attributed root-depleted families and 16.6% of root-enriched families. Among the root-enriched

cota A for 11.3% against 4.9%, and Planctomycetota for 8.3% against 2.4%. Myxococcota sensu
stricto account for 4.8% against 2.6%. Root-enriched and root-depleted families have unadjusted

admitted set. These detection frequencies remain sensitive to family prevalence.

gradient. These exploratory associations motivate analyses that account jointly for batch, spatial
dependence, compositionality and measurement limits.
