Machine learning-guided discovery of thermophilic carbonic anhydrases from environmental metagenomes

Corresponding author.

Contributed equally.

Abstract

Thermophilic carbonic anhydrases (CAs) are promising biocatalysts for carbon capture utilization and storage (CCUS) due to their stability and efficiency at elevated temperatures. This study presents a machine learning (ML)-guided approach to discover thermostable γ-class CA (γ-CA) from metagenomic datasets derived from Fang Hot Spring, Northern Thailand. To develop classification models, two sets of protein descriptors—dipeptide composition (DPC) and physicochemical/biochemical properties (AAindex)—were used to train classification models. Fourteen ML algorithms were systematically evaluated for each feature set. AdaBoost achieved the best performance for the DPC-based model, while LightGBM performed best with AAindex-based features. External validation with known CA sequences confirmed the ability of the models to discriminate thermophilic from non-thermophilic proteins. Applying the optimized models, we screened 1,534 predicted CAs and identified three high-confidence candidates (TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA). These were heterologously expressed in  E. coli , purified, and biochemically validated. All candidates exhibited carbonic anhydrase activity, trimeric oligomeric structures, and high melting temperatures ( T m  ranging from 97.0 °C to 109.1 °C). Although their hydration activity was modest compared to α-class CAs, their thermal robustness highlights their potential for industrial CO₂ capture. This study demonstrates an approach in which ML integrated with metagenomics enables efficient discovery and validation of robust enzymes from extreme environments, providing a scalable strategy for CCUS applications.

Introduction

Carbonic anhydrases (CAs) (EC 4.2.1.1) are a diverse group of metalloenzymes that catalyze the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide into bicarbonate and protons—a reaction that is essential to many biological processes. To date, CAs have been classified into eight distinct and evolutionarily unrelated families, including alpha (α), beta (β), gamma (γ), delta (δ), zeta (ζ), eta (η), theta (θ), and iota (ι) 1 – 3 . CAs offer multiple benefits in carbon capture processes, making them attractive biocatalysts for environmentally sustainable CO₂ mitigation strategies. Their rapid CO₂ hydration capabilities and energy efficiency contribute to improved performance over traditional chemical absorbents. However, because carbon capture utilization and storage (CCUS) often involve high-temperature industrial environments, thermostable CAs with enhanced longevity and stability are particularly crucial for effective and durable implementation 4 , 5 .

Among the eight known classes of CAs, the γ-class is generally regarded as the most thermostable 6 . This is attributed to its homotrimeric structure with typically Zn²⁺ ion coordinated, which features rigid, hydrophobic cores and stabilizing surface interactions that enhance thermal stability. The γ-CAs typically identified in thermophilic origins, such as Archaea and certain extremophilic bacteria including  Methanosarcina thermophila ,  Thermus thermophilus ,  Geobacillus kaustophilus ,  Aeribacillus pallidus ,  Persephonella hydrogeniphila ,  Persephonella marina ,  Thermosulfidibacter takaii , and  Pyrococcus horikoshii , all of which thrive in high-temperature environments (55–98 °C) 3 , 7 – 12 . Therefore, the γ-CAs are the most promising candidates for industrial applications under high-temperature conditions, such as in carbon capture from industrial flue gas and bio-based CO₂ conversion systems.

Particularly, thermophilic environments, such as hot springs, compost piles, and hydrothermal vents, are rich sources of extremophiles that produce thermostable and robust enzymes. However, these microbes are often difficult to culture, limiting direct access to their enzymatic potential. The metagenomic approach enables the discovery of novel enzymes, including thermophilic CAs from uncultured microbes especially those from extreme environments, which often possess highly robust and industrially valuable enzymes 13 – 17 . Recently, highly thermo-and alkali-stable CA-KR1 with a half-life of 24 h at 80 °C was discovered from metagenomic sample collected at a hot spring in Kirishima, Japan 18 .

Machine learning (ML) has significantly enhanced enzyme discovery from metagenomic datasets by providing efficient, scalable tools to explore the vast genetic diversity of uncultivated microorganisms. The computational frameworks that integrate structural and functional modeling have been developed to identify enzymes with unique catalytic activities from metagenomes 19 – 21 . Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of in identifying novel enzymes with desirable properties. The alkaline laccases from basidiomycete fungi were successfully discovered from metagenomic dataset using ML-integrated approach 22 . The Metagenome Cellulase Identification and Characterization (MCIC) tool, which combines sequence similarity with ML, was developed to identify and characterize cellulases from metagenomic data based on their temperature and pH profiles 23 . Recently, 21 novel PET-degrading enzymes were discovered using a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) for dataset augmentation and a pretrained Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM) language model to generate sequence embeddings for classification 24 . These examples underscore the impact of ML in enzyme discovery from massive metagenomic datasets, facilitating the identification of novel enzymes with tailored properties for various industrial applications.

Therefore, this work firstly reports the development and application of ML-based approach for the discovery of thermophilic γ-CAs from a metagenomic dataset constructed from Fang Hot Spring in Chiang Mai province, Thailand for potential use in the industrial Biobased-CCUS applications.

Results

γ-CA candidates

Fang Hot Spring is a high-temperature geothermal site in Doi Pha Hom Pok National Park in Fang District, Chiang Mai Province, Northern Thailand. Fang Hot Spring is considered a valuable thermophilic microbial resource for discovering thermostable enzymes due to its consistently high temperatures (51.5–94.2 °C) and mineral-rich ecological niche. Therefore, the metagenomic DNA libraries have been constructed from sediment samples and microbial mats collected from Fang Hot Spring, and the metagenomic sequencing was subsequently carried out. The metagenomic analysis showed considerable variation predicted in gene counts, ranging from 707,779 to 2,892,946 per sample. To identify the CA candidates using DIAMOND-Blastp against UniRef90 database, we found 1,534 predicted CAs from metagenomes including 6, 10, 1,214, and 303 putative CA candidates classified into α-class, β-class, γ-class, and unclassified-class, respectively. The high abundance of γ-CAs observed in Fang Hot Spring metagenome can be explained by the predominance of bacteria and archaea species in the sediment metagenomic datasets, in which these microbial taxa are known to possess γ-CA genes commonly 25 – 27 . The majority of these γ-CAs originated from  Pseudomonadati  (61.45%), followed by  Bacillati  (10.30%) and  Thermoproteati  (9.88%). A minor proportion belonged to methanogenic and thermophilic archaea, including  Methanobacteriati  (3.54%) and  Thermotogati  (2.22%), respectively.

Training data collection, feature extraction, and feature selection

The training dataset comprised amino acid sequences of γ-CA enzymes derived from cryothermal (low-temperature) and hydrothermal (high-temperature) environments. Before model training, sequence redundancy was removed using CD-HIT, resulting in 91 and 59 non-redundant sequences labeled as  CA hydrothermal  and  CA cryothermal , respectively. To investigate potential sequence features associated with environmental adaptation, conserved motifs across two enzyme classes were analyzed. Conserved motifs were illustrated by SeqLogo to visualize position-specific amino acid conservation distinguishing cryothermal- and hydrothermal-derived γ-CAs, representing key residues that may contribute to the protein feature descriptors used in ML models (Fig.  1 a).

Feature analysis of γ-CAs. ( a ) Conserved motifs between hydrothermal (upper) and cryothermal (lower) γ-CAs illustrated by SeqLogo. ( b ) PCA plots using all (left) and selected (right) dipeptide composition (DPC) features. ( c ) PCA plots using all (left) and selected (right) AAIndex features. Red and blue dots represent hydrothermal and cryothermal CAs, respectively.

To classify thermophilic γ-CAs, two distinct ML models were developed based on different sets of protein feature descriptors. The first model was constructed using Di-Peptide Composition (DPC) features, representing the frequency of all 400 possible dipeptide combinations. In contrast, the second model was developed on sets of physicochemical and biochemical properties, comprising 566 features derived from the AAindex database. These features represent various amino acid propensities such as hydrophobicity, polarity, molecular volume, and structural propensities. Both feature sets were normalized using min-max scaling, which rescaled all feature values to a uniform range from zero to one.

Subsequently, feature selection was performed to identify the most informative and discriminative features from each descriptor set. Five feature selection methods including, Chi-Square, Information Gain, F-score, Mutual Information, and Pearson Correlation Coefficient were employed to independently identify the top 20 most essential features from the dataset. To enhance robustness and minimize overfitting, only features consistently selected by at least 80% (4 out of 5) of these methods were retained. As a result, 18 features were selected for the DPC-based model and 8 features for the AAindex-based model (Table  S1 ). The discriminative power of the selected features was visualized by the Principal Component Analysis (PCA), presented in Fig.  1 (b) and Fig.  1 (c), which showed a separation between cryothermal and hydrothermal γ-CAs. The separation between the two groups in the selected feature plots demonstrates the effectiveness of feature selection in enhancing class discriminability.

Model development and γ-CA candidates’ selection

To classify thermophilic γ-CAs, two distinct ML models were constructed based on different types of protein feature descriptors. In this step, all selected features from the previous step were applied to construct the ML model using 14 different algorithms, including AdaBoost, Bagging, Decision Tree, Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees (GTDB), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), LightGBM, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Logistic Regression (LR), Multilayer Perceptron (MPL), Naive Bayes, Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and XGBoost. Model performance was assessed using four key metrics: sensitivity (Sn), specificity (Sp), accuracy (Acc), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), which are summarized in supplementary Table S2 and Fig.  2 .

Comparison of model performances trained with different feature descriptors. Radar plots illustrate the performance of 14 algorithms trained on ( a ) DPC and ( b ) AAIndex, evaluated by percentages of sensitivity (Sn, blue), specificity (Sp, red), accuracy (Acc, green), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC, orange).

For the model trained on DPC features, in terms of sensitivity, both Logistic Regression (LR) and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) achieved perfect scores of 100.00%, followed by Support Vector Machine (SVM) at 85.79%. For specificity, AdaBoost outperformed other models with a score of 64.40%, followed by LightGBM (61.21%) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) (59.70%). Accuracy-matric, AdaBoost also led with 74.00%, while Random Forest (RF), SVM, and XGBoost showed identical values at 72.67%. When comparing MCC, AdaBoost again demonstrated the highest performance with a coefficient of 0.451, closely followed by SVM (0.424) and RF (0.423). To identify the most balanced and robust model, a composite score was calculated by summing all four-performance metrics (Sn + Sp + Acc + %MCC). Based on this combined score, AdaBoost achieved the highest total (263.90%), indicating consistent performance across all metrics. Therefore, AdaBoost was selected as the classifier for thermophilic CA prediction using DPC-based features.

In parallel, the same set of ML algorithms was evaluated using features derived from the AAindex database to classify thermophilic γ-CAs. The model performances were assessed based on the same four metrics. Among all classifiers, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) achieved the highest sensitivity at 100.00%, followed by Support Vector Machine (SVM) with 87.78%, and Bagging with 84.56%. Naive Bayes demonstrated the highest specificity (69.70%), with LightGBM and AdaBoost following at 66.52% and 63.03%, respectively. In terms of accuracy, LightGBM outperformed all models with a value of 75.33%, followed by XGBoost (72.00%) and AdaBoost (71.33%). For MCC, LightGBM demonstrated the highest performance with a score of 0.4998, with XGBoost (0.4219) and Naive Bayes (0.4171) ranking closely behind. Based on all metrics, LightGBM achieved the highest overall score, indicating its highest performance across all evaluated criteria. Therefore, LightGBM was selected as the classifier in thermophilic CA prediction using AAIndex-based features. Additionally, the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and Precision-Recall Curve (PRC) were calculated to further evaluate the performance of the models (Figure  S1 ). The result exhibited that the AUROC values of the DPC-based AdaBoost model and the AAindex-based model were 0.7799 and 0.8216, respectively, while the corresponding AUPRC values were 0.8371 and 0.8602, reflecting a moderate discriminative capacity of both models (Figure  S1 ).

To further validate the robustness of the selected models, external validation was performed using known γ-CAs from thermophilic, mesophilic, and psychrophilic organisms (Table S3). The thermophilic CA ( WP_052721819 ) was correctly predicted with high hydrothermal probabilities (0.6301 with the AdaBoost-DPC model and 0.8643 with the LightGBM-AAindex model), while the mesophilic ( AAQ66301 ) and psychrophilic ( AFY49856 ) CAs showed lower probabilities. These results support the model performance to discriminate thermophilic from non-thermophilic γ-CAs.

To identify potential enzyme candidates, γ-CA sequences predicted from the Fang Hot Spring metagenomes were screened using the final AdaBoost-DPC and LightGBM-AAIndex models. Regarding the prediction, three high-confidence thermophilic γ-CA candidates were selected for further physiological and biochemical characterization: (1) TtCA, with the highest score from the DPC-based AdaBoost model; (2) CrCA, the highest-ranked candidate from the AAIndex-based LightGBM model; and (3) ToCA, a consensus candidate with high scores from both models.

Comparative amino acid sequence analysis of γ-CAs

Taxonomic classification of the CA-encoding contigs using BLASTN indicated that the best matches for TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA were  Thermus caldilimi  (79.96% identity),  Saccharopolyspora gregorii  (73.99% identity), and  Thermus oshimai  (99.57% identity), respectively. BLASTP analysis of the candidate genes against the UniRef90 database revealed that TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA share sequence similarity with γ-CAs from  Thermus thermamylovorans ,  Candidatus Riflebacteria  bacterium, and  Thermus oshimai  JL-2, with sequence identities of 90%, 62.90%, and 99.10%, respectively. In silico sequence and structural annotation of the three selected putative γ-CAs were then carried out to further in-dept understanding of their structure-functional relationships. Regarding amino acid sequence analysis, the coding sequence of the candidate γ-CAs,  TtCA ,  CrCA , and  ToCA  encoded polypeptides of 230, 177, and 229 amino acids with calculated molecular masses of 24.3, 19.2, and 24.2 kDa, respectively. Pairwise sequence comparisons showed that TtCA and ToCA shared a high degree of amino acid sequence conserved with 83% identity due to originating from a similar  Thermus  Genus. In contrast, CrCA exhibited unique residues, showing only 26% and 24% identity compared to TtCA and ToCA, respectively (Figure S2). The structural comparison with numerous crystallographic CA structures available in RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) demonstrated that both TtCA and ToCA have the highest homology to γ-CA from  Thermus thermophilus  HB8 (PDB: 6IVE) 10  (Figure S3(a), (c)) with 91% and 89% amino acid sequence identity, respectively (Table S4). While CrCA showed only 39% identity to putative carbonic anhydrase from  Clostridium difficile  (Cam; PDB: 4MFG), indicating significant structural divergence (Figure S3(b), Table S4). The conserved amino acid residues Arg59, Asp61, and Asp76, which are involved in trimeric structure formation in  Methanosarcina thermophila  (PDB: 1QRG), are also present in TtCA and ToCA. In contrast, Asp76 is replaced by Glu59 in CrCA. The catalytic Zn 2+  bound to three histidines, as shown in the red star (Figure S3) is strictly conserved across all three γ-CAs, as is the other γ-CAs. Despite these differences, the catalytic Zn 2+  binding site, coordinated by three conserved histidine residues (red stars in Figure S3), is strictly preserved across all candidate γ-CAs and other known γ-CAs.

Protein expression and purification

To evaluate whether the metagenome-derived candidate CA genes,  TtCA ,  CrCA , and  ToCA  encode active CA enzymes, the three candidate genes were synthesized and heterologously expressed under T7 promoter in  E. coli  BL21(DE3). Soluble protein expression was optimized by varying induction parameters, including cell density, IPTG concentration, and induction time. Regarding the results, recombinant TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA were successfully produced as soluble forms under the optimized conditions at cell density reached 1.0 OD 600  with 0.5 mM IPTG at 25 °C and continuous shaking at 200 rpm. Maximum soluble protein yield of recombinant TtCA was achieved after 3 h of induction, whereas high soluble protein for CrCA and ToCA were observed after 24 h of induction (Fig.  3 (a)). After that, the induced cell was prepared on a large scale for protein purification. The soluble CA proteins were purified by Ni 2+ -NTA affinity chromatography with C-terminal His-tag on a pET28a(+) vector. The final yield of purified proteins was around 2 mg/mL with 95% purity. The molecular weights of TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA analyzed by SDS-PAGE were approximately 25, 23, and 30 kDa, respectively (Fig.  3 (b)). The purified proteins were subsequently used to characterize CA activity.

Characterization of CA properties

The quaternary structures of the candidate CA enzymes were analyzed using Native-PAGE, as previously described 28 . The recombinant CAs expressed in  E. coli  BL21(DE3) exhibited approximate molecular weights of 120 kDa for TtCA and ToCA and 100 kDa for CrCA (Fig.  4 (a)), suggesting the trimeric structure. A consistent result was obtained from dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Figure S4), where the dominant quaternary structure of CrCA was predicted to be approximately 181.75 kDa (Figure S3). A protonogram analysis was performed to assess the candidate CA enzymes’ hydratase qualitatively. As described in the experimental section, the purified CA proteins were loaded onto the gel and run under denaturing with non-reducing conditions. One half of SDS-PAGE was stained with Coomassie blue, while the other half was stained with bromothymol blue as part of the protonogram protocol. The Coomassie blue-stained gel (Fig.  4 (b), left) exhibited distinct bands corresponding to TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA monomeric forms, with apparent molecular weights of 25, 23, and 30 kDa, respectively. Meanwhile, the bromothymol blue-stained gel (Fig.  4 (b), right) initially appeared blue in its deprotonated form. Upon exposure to CO 2 , the bands changed to yellow due to protonation during the CO 2  hydration reaction. This color shift confirmed that the three candidate CA enzymes exhibited CA activity.

Determination of quaternary structure of CA protein by Native-PAGE ( a ) and analysis of carbonic anhydrase activtiy by protonography ( b ). “M” stands for the protein marker. The red arrows indicate carbonic anhydrase activity.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) was employed to thermostability, as it directly detects conformational changes in proteins that impact stability. The melting temperature ( T m ) was defined as the midpoint at which the protein population unfolds. The results revealed that all candidate CAs exhibited extremely high  T m  values compared to previously characterized CAs (Table S5). Specifically, TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA showed  T m  values of 101.5 °C, 109.1 °C, and 97.0 °C, respectively (Table  1 ). Additionally, carbon dioxide hydration activity was assessed using a colorimetric CO 2  hydration assay adapted from Kim & Jo, 2022 29  with activity expressed in Wilbur-Anderson units (WAU). The CA activity of TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA exhibited 3.21 ± 0.1, 4.06 ± 0.19 and 4.56 ± 0.2 WAU/mg, respectively (Table  1 ). In contrast, bovine carbonic anhydrase (bCA), used as a positive control, demonstrated significantly higher activity at 11,004 ± 955 WAU/mg.

Discussion

This study is the first to report on a ML-guided approach for discovering thermophilic enzymes, specifically γ-CAs, from vast metagenomic datasets. The structure-based comparison approach remains limited for γ-CAs discovery due to the scarcity of experimentally resolved protein structures. Moreover, although γ-CA homologs frequently exhibit high structural-level similarity, not all possess catalytic activity for CO₂ hydration 35 . Therefore, this complicates the functional prediction of γ-CAs when relying solely on structural comparison. To address these challenges, the ML pipelines that integrate sequence-derived features with physicochemical and biochemical properties have been developed. Compared with structure-based methods, sequence-driven models can capture functional and stability-related patterns even when structural data are lacking. While structural modeling is valuable for interpreting catalytic mechanisms, sequence-based ML provides broader scalability, enabling high-throughput screening of large metagenomic libraries. Thus, both approaches are complementary, but sequence-based ML offers a practical advantage for early-stage discovery when structural information is scarce.

Recent advances in machine learning have significantly enhanced enzyme discovery from metagenomic datasets by leveraging sequence–function correlations to identify novel candidates that remain undetected by conventional homology-based searches 20 , 36 . ML-driven approaches have proven particularly effective in detecting thermophilic enzymes, as demonstrated: Shahraki et al. 37  applied a sequence-based classifier to identify three thermostable xylanases from sheep and cow rumen metagenomes, and Zhang et al. 38  employed a deep-learning model to identify a thermophilic chitinase from a marine metagenome. These highlight the utility of ML in discovering robust enzymes from extreme environments. Compared to traditional homology-based, ML-guided approaches increase the success of discovering truly thermostable enzymes. These studies highlight the utility of ML in discovering robust enzymes from extreme environments. Nevertheless, the relatively small size of our training dataset constrained the use of advanced deep learning frameworks that now represent the state of the art in protein thermostability prediction. As a result, we primarily relied on traditional machine learning algorithms, leading to moderate accuracy compared with recent deep learning models 39 , 40 . Even with these constraints, our ML-based pipeline successfully pinpointed multiple γ-CA candidates with exceptional thermal stability, demonstrating the practical value of this approach for enzyme discovery from extreme environments.

The candidate γ-CA genes,  TtCA ,  CrCA  and  ToCA , were effectively identified from the Fang Hot Spring metagenome using the developed AdaBoost DPC-based and LightGBM AAIndex-based ML-based approaches. These genes were successfully expressed as an oligomeric structure and purified for thermostability and carbonic anhydrase activity testing. Notably, this is the first study to evaluate the stability of γ-CAs using the denaturation midpoint ( T m ). Regarding melting temperature ( T m ) analysis using DSC, the recombinant TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA exhibited higher thermostability with  T m  values ranging from 97.0 to 109.1 °C (Table  1 ), compared to previously reported α-CAs and β-CAs (Table S5). The previously published animal α-CAs, including human carbonic anhydrase (h-CA I) and bovine carbonic anhydrase (bCA II) exhibited low thermostability with  T m  value ranging from 54.5–64.0°C 30 , 41 – 43 , while bacterial α-CA (PmCA (sp-)) isolated from  Sulfurihydrogenibium yellowstonense  YO3AOP1 is highly thermostable ( T m  84.5 °C) 44 , 45 . These results confirm that TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA are hyperthermostable CA, highlighting the effectiveness of our ML pipeline in identifying thermostable CA from the Fang Hot Spring metagenomic libraries.

In examining the sequence and structural features of our candidate γ-CAs (TtCA, ToCA, and CrCA), we compared them with the well-characterized Cam from  Methanosarcina thermophila . Cam has been shown to rely on conserved residues Arg59, Asp61, and Asp76 for trimer formation, where Arg59 and Asp61 form an intra-monomer salt bridge that stabilizes individual subunits, and Asp76 contributes an inter-monomer salt bridge essential for maintaining the trimeric assembly. Sequence alignment revealed that these residues are conserved in TtCA and ToCA, and are also present in CrCA except for a substitution of Asp76 with Glu. Nevertheless, native-PAGE and DLS analyses confirmed that all three candidate enzymes form stable trimers, indicating that the inter-monomer interaction is likely preserved in CrCA despite this substitution. These results suggest that TtCA, ToCA, and CrCA, like Cam, assemble into stable trimeric complexes through conserved salt bridge interactions that not only support quaternary structure but may also contribute to thermostability. Beyond structural features, annotation also highlighted the catalytic residue Glu62 in Cam, which has been implicated in CO₂ hydration catalysis. Consistent with previous reports, γ-CAs generally exhibit lower activity than α-CAs, with Cam representing the highest γ-CA activity reported so far (4872.2 WAU/mg), yet still well below that of α-CAs such as bovine CA (11,004 ± 955 WAU/mg). In contrast, some thermostable γ-CAs, including that from  Thermus thermophilus  HB8, show very limited activity (9.88 WAU/mg). Our findings place TtCA, ToCA, and CrCA within this latter category, as they exhibited similarly low activities of 3.21, 4.06, and 4.56 WAU/mg, respectively, underscoring that while trimeric assembly is conserved, catalytic efficiency among thermostable γ-CAs remains limited.

In summary, this approach enables the effective identification and prioritization of thermophilic γ-CAs from complex and massive metagenomic data, offering a scalable and robust solution for enzyme discovery from extreme environments.

Methods

This study consists of three main steps to discover thermophilic CAs from metagenomes (Fig.  5 ). First, putative CA candidates were identified from our in-house metagenomic dataset using bioinformatics analysis. Second, ML models were developed to predict and prioritize potential thermophilic CAs from the initial candidates. Finally, the selected CA candidates were experimentally validated for their enzymatic activity and thermostability.

Overall workflow for ML-guided discovery of thermophilic γ-CAs from metagenomes. The workflow includes three main steps: (1) metagenomic analysis for predicting and selecting γ-CA candidates; (2) ML model training using curated CA sequences with feature extraction, selection, and evaluation across 14 algorithms; and (3) candidate validation including selection of three predicted thermophilic γ-CAs, host expression, protein investigation, and functional and thermal characterization using CO₂ hydration assay and scanning calorimetry (DSC).

Prediction of γ-CA candidates from In-house metagenome sequences

Our in-house metagenome dataset was obtained from Fang Hot Spring in Chiang Mai province, Thailand, comprising sediment and biomat samples across a temperature gradient of 50–90 °C. Raw sequencing data were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under project number PRJNA1223108. The raw reads were performed the quality control using fastp 46  by trimming barcode sequences and filtering low quality sequences (Phred quality scores < 30). Cleaned reads were then assembled into contigs using metaSPAdes 47  with a minimum contig length of 500 bp. Gene prediction was performed on the assembled contigs by metaProdigal 48 , retaining predicted genes with a minimum length of 100 bp. Functional annotation was then performed by homology search using DIAMOND 49  against the UniRef90 database 50  with version 2022_02. Candidates of γ-CA enzyme were identified based on the annotation results.

Collection of training data and feature extraction

Datasets used for model training and evaluation in this study consisted of two distinct classes: cryothermal CAs and hydrothermal CAs. Amino acid sequences of cryothermal enzymes were obtained from publicly available metagenomes derived from Antarctic soil and ice, whereas the hydrothermal enzyme sequences were collected from the metagenome of hydrothermal vents 51 . Sequences containing non-canonical amino acids were excluded before further analysis. Redundancy was minimized by clustering the amino acid sequences at 100% similarity using CD-HIT 52 . The training data is available at  https://github.com/wutto/thermophillicCA .

Protein feature descriptors used for model training were extracted using Biopython 53 , which computed features based on the compositions and properties of amino acid sequences. These descriptors were categorized into two types: (1) Di-Peptide Composition (DPC), comprising 400 features representing the frequency of each possible dipeptide pair, and (2) Physicochemical and Biochemical Properties, consisting of 566 features derived from the AAindex database 54 . Feature normalization was performed using min–max scaling to standardize all features values to a range between 0 and 1. The normalization was calculated using the following formula:

The most informative features were selected by combining the top-ranked features from five independent feature selection methods including Chi-Square, Information Gain, F-score, Mutual Information, and Pearson Correlation Coefficient. Features identified by at least 80% (4 out of 5) of these methods were used for building the classification models. The feature normalization and selection were performed by the iLearnPlus 55  platform.

Model development and selection of thermophilic γ-CA candidates

To determine the most effective model for classifying thermophilic γ-CAs, a systematic approach was employed using the iLearnPlus-AutoML module. Two models were developed based on distinct protein descriptor sets including dipeptide composition (DPC) and physicochemical and biochemical properties. In this study, fourteen classification algorithms were applied, including AdaBoost, Bagging, Decision Tree, Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees (GTDB), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), LightGBM, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Logistic Regression (LR), Multilayer Perceptron (MPL), Naive Bayes, Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and XGBoost. To measure and compare the performance of models, a 5-fold cross-validation strategy was implemented to minimize overfitting and verify consistent performance across data subsets. To further validate the robustness of the selected models, external validation was performed using known γ-CAs from thermophilic ( WP_052721819 ), mesophilic ( AAQ66301 ), and psychrophilic ( AFY49856 )57 organisms 30 , 56 , 57  (Table S3). To identify final thermophilic CA candidates, γ-CA sequences predicted from our in-house metagenome were selected using two distinct ML models developed in this study, one based on DPC and the other on physicochemical and biochemical properties. From these predictions, three top-ranking thermophilic γ-CA candidates were selected based on the highest confidence scores; one from each individual model and one optimized across both models. To validate the presence of characteristic CA domains, the selected candidates were predicted for their protein domain using InterProScan5 58 .

Candidate CA gene synthesis

The candidate CA genes TtCA (accession number PV553289), CrCA (accession number PV553290), and ToCA (accession number PV553291) were designed and produced by GenScript (Singapore), a company specializing in gene synthesis. The gene contains NcoI and XhoI restriction sites at 5’ and 3’ ends, respectively. The codon usage of the candidate CA genes was optimized for  E. coli  expression and ligated into the expression vector pET28a(+) with 6xHis-tags at C-terminal.

Sequence analysis and structure prediction

The size of the candidate CA proteins (kDa) was calculated by AAT Bioquest 59 . Sequence alignment and structure prediction were analyzed by FoldScript 1.0 60  and Phyre webserver 61 , respectively.

Optimization of CA protein expression

The recombinant plasmids harboring the candidate CA genes were transformed into  E. coli  BL21(DE3). The starter culture for protein expression was prepared in 5 mL of Luria-Bertani broth (LB) containing 1.0% tryptone, 1.0% NaCl, and 0.5% yeast extract supplemented with 50 µg/mL of kanamycin. The culture was then grown at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 18 h. Protein expression was performed by inoculating 2.0% of starter culture (1 mL) into 50 mL LB supplemented with 50 µg/mL of kanamycin with continuous shaking until an optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reaches 0.6 to 1.0. CA protein expression was induced by adding Isopropyl ß-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) with varying concentrations at 0.1 and 0.5 mM, followed by culturing at 25 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The induced cells were harvested after 1, 3, and 24 h of induction for subsequent protein expression analysis.

Validation of CA protein expression

Two milliliters of induced cells with various culturing conditions were collected by centrifugation. The bacterial cells were resuspended in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, with a final concentration of 0.005 OD/µL, then lysed by an ultrasonicator with ten cycles at an amplitude of 60 (each cycle consisting of 10 s on and 10 s off). Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged to separate the soluble and insoluble protein fractions. SDS-PAGE analysis was performed by mixing the soluble and insoluble protein fractions with 5x protein loading dye (containing 60 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 25% glycerol, 2% SDS, 14.4 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 1% bromophenol blue). The samples were then boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of soluble and insoluble protein fractions, corresponding to 0.04 OD per well, were loaded onto a 12% polyacrylamide gel. The gel was run at 25 mA for 45 min.

Purification of CA protein

The candidate CA genes were expressed under the optimal condition in 1 L of culture medium. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,600 ×g. Recombinant proteins were extracted by sonication in lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH 7.4) using 10 ml cell culture/ml lysis buffer. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 17,400 ×g for 60 min. Supernatants were purified by Ni 2+ -NTA affinity chromatography using the HisPrep FF 16/10 column (GE Healthcare, Uppsala, Sweden) equilibrated with lysis buffer. The His-tagged proteins were eluted using an imidazole gradient of 20–300 mM in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.4. The eluent fraction containing the target protein was pooled, buffer-exchanged with 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.4 containing 10% glycerol, and then concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units with a 10-kDa cut-off (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Protein concentration was measured by NanoDrop spectrophotometer at 280 nm on the UV spectrum using a mass extinction coefficient (12.95 mM −1  cm −1 , 19.94 mM −1  cm-1, and 11.46 mM −1  cm −1  for TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA) determined by quantitative amino acid analysis from ExPASY ( http://web.expasy.org/protparam/ ). The protein profile was analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

Protonogram analysis

Protonography was performed as described by Prete 62 . Samples of TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA were prepared by mixing 8 µg of purified CA protein with protein loading dye (0.0625 M Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% glycerol, 1.0% SDS, 0.001% bromophenol blue) and without boiling. The gel was run at 150 V until the dye front exited the gel. After electrophoresis, the gel was soaked in 2.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h on a shaker and then washed twice in 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.2, containing 10% isopropanol for 10 min. Subsequently, the gel was incubated in 0.1% bromothymol blue in 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.2 for 30 min. To visualize the hydratase activity, the gel was then immersed in CO 2 -saturated water, prepared by bubbling CO 2  into 200 mL of distilled water for 3 hours 63 .

Colorimetric CO 2  hydration assay

The CO 2  hydration activity of CA was determined using a spectrophotometer by a colorimetric CO 2  hydration assay adapted from Kim & Jo, 2022 29 . The reaction was performed at 25 °C in a 96-well plate containing 20 µL of purified enzyme (~ 20 µg) and 100 µL of 50 mM sodium phosphate pH 8.0 with 500 µM bromothymol blue. After stabilizing the pH, 80 µL of CO 2 -saturated water was introduced into mixture, prepared by bubbling gaseous CO 2  through deionized water. The absorbance at optical density 620 nm was immediately measured in real-time for 2 min. The time ( t ) required for the absorbance change from 1.8 to 2.8 was monitored where  t o  and  t  represent uncatalyzed and enzyme-catalyzed reactions, respectively. The CO 2  hydration activity was calculated in WAU/mg enzyme units. All measurements were performed in triplicate. Bovine carbonic anhydrase (Sigma-Aldrich) was performed as a positive control.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The melting temperature ( T m ) of carbonic anhydrase was determined using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) with a DSC 214 Polyma instrument (NETZSCH, Wittelsbacherstrasse, Selb, Germany). The purified TtCA, CrCA, and ToCA proteins were diluted in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.4 to a protein concentration of 52 µM. A sample volume of 10 µL was scanned using DSC from 20 to 150 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C/min. The  T m  values of the samples were verified as the mid-point on the DSC curves.

Conclusion

This study highlights the integration of metagenomics approach and ML for the discovery of thermophilic γ-CAs from the extreme ecological niche. The developed AdaBoost-DPC and LightGBM-AAIndex models effectively predicted sequence features and biochemical characteristics of the candidate γ-CAs from massive metagenomic datasets, enhancing the precision and scalability of enzyme screening, demonstrating an effective pipeline for mining thermostable enzymes. The approach provides a robust pipeline for identifying thermostable γ-CAs advancing their potential application in industrial CO₂ capture processes.
