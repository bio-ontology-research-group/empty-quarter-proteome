17              ABSTRACT
18              Rhodopsin with the NTQ motif and heliorhodopsins, both discovered for the first time in
19    actinomycetes of the family Geodermatophilaceae were characterized, along with characterization of the
20    DTEW, DTEF and NDQ rhodopsins reported in members of this family previously. The identified
21    absorption maxima ranged from 513 to 559 nm, of which the blue-shifted rhodopsin contained the NTQ
22    motif and the red-shifted one were heliorhodopsin. An assessment of the pumping activity of the NTQ
23    rhodopsin and heliorhodopsin showed that the former functioned as inward Cl --, Br--, I--pump with much
24    weaker activity towards NO3-, while no any activity was detected for the later. The DTEW and DTEF
25    rhodopsins had the outward H+-transport activity which was dependent on the presence of Ca 2+ ions and
26    on the particular E. coli expression strain. The outward pumping of H+ was also detected for NDQ
27    rhodopsin both in NaCl and KCl solutions at pH 5 and 6, but not at pH 7. Weak Na +-efflux activity for this
28    rhodopsin was observed at pH 6 and 7 in NaCl solution only after adding carbonyl cyanide m-
29    chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP).

41              INTRODUCTION
42              Rhodopsins are a large family of integral membrane proteins containing retinal chromophores that
43    perform the photoreceptor function and also the function of ion pumps in the processes of light energy
44    conversion. Rhodopsins are classified into Type I microbial and Type II animal rhodopsins (play a key role
45    in animal vision). Microbial and animal rhodopsins contain all-trans and 11-cis retinal molecules,
46    respectively, that capture light (Rozenberg et al., 2021). Recently, a new family of rhodopsins,
47    heliorhodopsins (HeR), was discovered by the functional metagenome analysis, and their global
48    distribution in Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya and viruses was shown (Pushkarev et al., 2018; Shihoya et al.,
49    2019; Hososhima et al., 2022). Heliorhodopsins are distant relatives of Type I microbial rhodopsins, and
50    their most distinct difference from conventional rhodopsins is inverted membrane topology (Pushkarev et
51    al., 2018; Kovalev et al., 2020; Shihoya et al., 2019).
52              It is worth noting that most studies within the last two decades have focused on discovery and
53    characterization of various microbial rhodopsins from marine and freshwater microorganisms (Chazan et
54    al., 2022; Fuhrman et al., 2008; Chuon et al., 2021; Keffer et al., 2015; Nakamura et al., 2016; Sharma et
55    al., 2008; Dwulit-Smith et al., 2018; Pushkarev and Béjà, 2016; Pushkarev et al., 2018; Hososhima et al.,
56    2022; Singh et al., 2023). Little is known about distribution and the biological role of these proteins in
57    microorganisms inhabiting terrestrial environments, especially extreme ones. By metagenomic approaches
58    the rhodopsins were revealed, for instance, in phyllospheres of plants, soil crust and Antarctic Dry Valley
59    edaphic systems (Atamna-Ismaeel et al., 2012; Finkel et al., 2013; Guerrero et al., 2017). Rhodopsins
60    were also found in some cultured prokaryotes from terrestrial ecosystems, such as Exiguobacterium
61    sibiricum from Siberian permafrost (Petrovskaya et al., 2010; Dioumaev et al., 2013), the thylakoid-
62    lacking cyanobacterium Gloeobacter violaceus from calcareous rock (Choi et al., 2014), and
63    Sphingomonas paucimobilis from hospital respirator (Maliar et al., 2020). The rhodopsins from the

64    aforementioned organisms have been well characterized and shown to possess H +-pumping activity. A Cl--
65    pumping rhodopsin was found in cyanobacterium Mastigocladopsis repens, a soil isolate (Hasemi et al.,
66    2016).
67              Recently, the presence of rhodopsins was reported in actinomycetes (formerly called
68    actinobacteria) of the family Geodermatophilaceae (class Actinomycetes, phylum Actinomycetota) which
69    originate primarily from hot and arid terrestrial environments (Tarlachkov et al., 2020; Tarlachkov et al.,
70    2023). By using a bioinformatics-based approach, the rhodopsins with DTE and NDQ amino acid motifs
71    in positions 85, 89 and 96 (in BR numbering) were discovered in members of this family. The cluster
72    analysis was further showed that the DTE rhodopsin group consisted of two separate groups, DTEW and
73    DTEF, which differed in the presence of highly conserved tryptophan (DTEW) and phenylalanine (DTEF)
74    at position 182 (in BR numbering). Thus, three different groups of microbial rhodopsins were identified in
75    Geodermatophilaceae before the present work, namely, DTEW, DTEF and NDQ (Tarlachkov et al., 2020;
76    Tarlachkov et al., 2023).
77              The DTEW rhodopsins are well known and occur in various groups of microorganisms, including
78    actinomycetes (Chuon et al., 2021; Keffer et al., 2015; Nakamura et al., 2016). The DTEF rhodopsins, on
79    the contrary, are quite unique and have been found so far only in representatives of Geodermatophilaceae.
80    As for the rhodopsins with NDQ motif, these have not been described in the literature among
81    actinomycetes           and      other      gram-positives           (monoderms),            except       for     a     representative            of
82    Geodermatophilaceae (Tarlachkov et al., 2020), although some close protein sequences of actinomycete
83    origin are present in public protein databases. However, the physiological role and functional
84    characteristics of rhodopsins from Geodermatophilaceae have not been studied.
85              Although generally considered endemic to soils, evolution of members of Geodermatophilaceae
86    has continued in specialized biotopes, including rocks and desert sandy soils in areas of moisture
87    deficiency, low nutrients, high salinity and high UV- and gamma-radiation (Busarakam et al., 2016;
88    Tarlachkov et al., 2023; Urzì et al., 2001). The rhodopsin-containing Geodermatophilaceae inhabiting
89    such specialized land biotopes represent unique group for study and broadening our understanding of the
90    diversity and functions of microbial rhodopsins and their role in biological adaptation to extremal
91    environments.
92              In this work, we provide new data on the diversity and properties of rhodopsins from
93    actinomycetes of the family Geodermatophilaceae.

 95              MATERIALS AND METHODS
 96              Bioinformatic analysis
 97              Complete and high-quality draft genome sequences of 110 bacteria of the family
 98    Geodermatophilaceae were obtained from GenBank, (Supplementary Table S1) and re-annotated using
 99    Prokka 1.14.6 (Seemann, 2014) with the Barrnap 0.9 plug-in. In order to search and identify the
100    rhodopsins genes present in the genomes studied, the reference protein sequences of well-known
101    rhodopsins were used as a query in BLASTp search against translated CDS which were extracted from
102    each genome. A best-hits with e-values lower than 0.01 were considered as potential rhodopsin proteins.
103    Then, each found sequence was aligned using MUSCLE (Edgar, 2021) against reference sequences to
104    finally identify rhodopsins.
105              Multiple sequence alignment of the revealed rhodopsin proteins was performed by MUSCLE.
106    Phylogenetic trees were inferred by MEGA11 (Tamura et al., 2021). The pairwise identity between protein
107    sequences was determined using TaxonDC 1.3 (Tarlachkov and Starodumova, 2017). The sequences
108    closest to the found rhodopsins were identified by BLASTp searches against the NCBI non-redundant
109    protein database (NCBI NR).
110
111              Genome sequencing and assembly
112              The cells of strain Geodermatophilus sp. DSM 44513 were grown at 28 °С for 24 hours on a rotary
113    shaker in a peptone yeast medium containing the following (g/L): peptone, 5; yeast extract, 3; K 2HPO4,
114    0.2; glucose, 5; NaCl, 3; pH 7. To obtain long-reads, DNA was isolated with GeneJET Genomic DNA
115    Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). To enhance cells lysis, enzymatic cell wall digestion was
116    performed in buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl 8.0, 0.05 mM EDTA, 40 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100,
117    and supplemented with 1 mg/ml lysozyme and 0.09 mg/ml EndoRB49 (Chernyshov et. al, 2023). Library
118    were prepared using Ligation Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) and Native Barcoding
119    Expansion 13-24 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK). The resulting library was sequenced with
120    MinION flow cell R9.4.1 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK).
121              For obtaining short-reads, DNA isolation was performed using a FastDNA SPIN Kit and FastPrep-
122    24 homogenizer (MP Biomedicals, USA). Library was prepared with a KAPA HyperPlus Kit (KAPA,
123    USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with a NovaSeq 6000 S2
124    Reagent Kit (200 cycles).
125              Long reads basecalling was performed using Guppy v6.5.7 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK).
126    Short and low-quality reads were removed using Filtlong v0.2.1 (https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong).
127    Genome backbone was assembled using Flye v2.9.2 (Kolmogorov et. al, 2019). Adapter sequences and

133              Rhodopsins cloning and expression

142    0.5 mM for E. coli C43(DE3) for 4 hours at 37 °С with the addition of retinal to a final concentration of

149              Protein purification and absorption measurements
150              All procedures were carried out at 4 °C. Harvested cells were resuspended in a buffer containing
151    50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.8, and 300 mM NaCl, followed by ultrasonication. Undisrupted cells
152    were removed by centrifugation at 5,000g for 10 min. Further, membrane fraction was sedimented at
153    100,000g for 1 hour, and membrane proteins were extracted using the same buffer supplemented with 1%
154    DDM (n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside). Purification of rhodopsins was carried out on Ni-IDA agarose (Biontex)
155    according to the manufacturer's recommendations. After elution, buffer was changed to 50 mM Tris-HCl,
156    pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.05% DDM using Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters. Purity was confirmed by
157    electrophoresis under denaturing conditions. Absorption spectra were recorded with a Shimadzu UV 1800
158    spectrophotometer.

160              Pumping activity assay
161              Pelleted cells were washed three times with appropriate unbuffered salt solution at a concentration
162    of 100 mM, and then re-suspended for ion transport measurements. The sample was illuminated by 500W
163    halogen lamp supplemented with IR filter. The pH changes were measured in the thermostated cell at 20
164    °C with intensive stirring and recorded in time-dependent manner. CCCP (carbonyl cyanide m-
165    chlorophenyl hydrazone) at final concentration of 30 μM was used to verify and discriminate proton and
166    non-proton pumping activities.

168              RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
169              New groups of rhodopsins
170              As mentioned previously, three groups of rhodopsins with DTEW, DTEF, and NDQ motifs were
171    found in members of the family Geodermatophilaceae by bioinformatic analysis (Tarlachkov et al., 2020).
172    A more extended analysis of the available genome sequences of 110 members of this family, including
173    Geodermatophilus sp. DSM 44513, sequenced in this work, revealed 69 strains containing the
174    aforementioned DTEW, DTEF, and NDQ rhodopsins, and also NTQ rhodopsin (presumably Cl - pump)
175    and heliorhodopsins. Thus, five groups of rhodopsins have been discovered in members of
176    Geodermatophilaceae to date (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1, S2).

190              NTQ rhodopsin
191              The aforementioned novel rhodopsin with a NTQ motif at positions 85, 89 and 96 (in BR
192    numbering) from Geodermatophilus sp. DSM 44513 (GspTR) formed a distinct branch on the unrooted
193    tree of rhodopsins found in representatives of the family Geodermatophilaceae (Fig. 1). This rhodopsin
194    also clustered apart from the clades of previously reported NTQ rhodopsins acting primarily as Cl - pumps
195    (Supplementary Fig. S3).
196              To date, only three NTQ rhodopsins have been characterized in detail, namely, NM-R3 from
197    Nonlabens marinus (Flavobacteriia) (Yoshizawa et al., 2014), FR from Fulvimarina pelagi
198    (Alphaproteobacteria) (Inoue et al., 2014) and AaClR from Alteribacter aurantiacus (Bacillaceae) (Singh
199    et al., 2023). In addition, two identified but incompletely characterized rhodopsins from this group have
200    been reported: PoClR from Parvularcula oceani (Alphaproteobacteria) (Inoue et al., 2016) and unnamed
201    protein from Nonlabens sp. YIK11 (Flavobacteriia) (Kwon et al., 2016). There is also information on
202    some uncharacterized NTQ rhodopsins originating from marine bacteria (Yoshizawa et al., 2014, Kwon et
203    al., 2016). All the above bacteria with NTQ rhodopsins, except for Alteribacter aurantiacus and
204    Geodermatophilus sp. DSM 44513, are Gram-negatives (diderms), and all originate from aquatic, mainly
205    marine, environments, with the exception of the above Geodermatophilus sp. DSM 44513 isolated from a
206    terrestrial source (marble quarry) (Urzì et al., 2001).
207              In the NCBI protein database, the sequences nearest to the NTQ rhodopsin from
208    Geodermatophilus sp. DSM 44513 (GspTR) were found in uncultivated actinomycetes, such as a
209    representative of the suborder Frankineae from biological soil crust sampled in Negev Desert (GenBank
210    acc. CAA9312725.1, 79.6% identity) and a member of the order Mycobacteriales from soil (GenBank
211    acc. HWG93181.1, 63.0% identity). Other found rhodopsins showed less than 43% identities to GspTR
212    and did not always contain NTQ motif. Thus, the phylogenetically distant GspTR rhodopsin discovered in
213    Geodermatophilus sp. DSM 44513 and some related satellite sequences from uncultivated organisms
214    available in the NCBI protein database form a separate group of hypothetical Cl - pumps which appear to
215    specialize for non-aquatic ecological niches.
216              GspTR rhodopsin and its homologs from the two above non-aquatic actinomycetes, members of
217    Frankineae and Mycobacteriales, shared less than 38.9% amino acid identity to the all other described
218    NTQ rhodopsins from aquatic bacteria and had several noticeable differences in key amino acids. For
219    example, GspTR contained a hydrophobic valine at position 204 (in BR numbering), while the NTQ
220    rhodopsins from the above aquatic bacteria had a charged arginine at homologous positions
221    (Supplementary Fig. S4). As a result of such substitution, the H+ release mechanisms may alter due to the
222    possible involvement of the corresponding amino acid residues in the proton-releasing group (PRG).

223              Heliorhodopsins
224              A total of 18 heliorhodopsin genes were discovered in actinomycetes of the family
225    Geodermatophilaceae by the bioinformatic analysis (Supplementary Table S1, S2). These were present
226    predominantly in strains of the genus Modestobacter and also identified in three Blastococcus strains and
227    three representatives of Geodermatophilus. The detected heliorhodopsins were assigned to subfamily 1
228    according to the classification of Kovalev et al. (Kovalev et al., 2020) and to group A according to Chazan
229    et al. (Chazan et al., 2022). The subfamily 1 and group A contain mostly proteins from actinomycetes,
230    including HeR 48C12, a well-studied member of the heliorhodopsin family found in an actinomycete from
231    the freshwater Lake Kinneret (Pushkarev et al., 2018).
232              The identified Geodermatophilaceae heliorhodopsins often co-occurred with NDQ rhodopsins in
233    the same strains (Supplementary Table S1, S2). In two strains, M. roseus DSM 45764 and M. marinus
234    DSM 45201, the genes for heliorhodopsins and NDQ-rhodopsins were closely located, but did not form an
235    operon (Supplementary Fig. S5). The heliorhodopsin genes in Blastococcus sp. DSM 46792,
236    Modestobacter sp. VKM Ac-2978 and M. caceresii KNN 45-2b were frame-shifted or fragmented
237    (Supplementary Fig. S6), in contrast to all other rhodopsin genes in members of the family
238    Geodermatophilaceae, and are most likely inactive. The above findings suggest a weaker selection
239    pressure for the heliorhodopsin genes as compared with the other rhodopsins, at least in
240    Geodermatophilaceae. On the other hand, the heliorhodopsin proteins of Geodermatophilaceae have a
241    relatively large percentage of identity among themselves, typically more than 85%, which may point to
242    some conservation processes.
243              Heliorhodopsins from the family Geodermatophilaceae showed less than 49% identity with HeR
244    48C12, the aforementioned well-studied heliorhodopsin from actinomycete (Pushkarev et al., 2018). But
245    at the same time, the Geodermatophilaceae heliorhodopsins have conserved most of the reported
246    functionally important amino acids (Supplementary Fig. S7). Notable differences in amino acid sequences
247    were revealed at positions associated with dimerization and the AB loop, and also with serine-to-alanine
248    substitution at position 76 (in 48C12 numbering), which is part of the retinal Schiff base (RSB).
249              To date, there is no consensus on the biological and functional roles of heliorhodopsins. One of the
250    approaches to understanding their role is the analysis of neighboring genes. From the strongest association
251    between heliorhodopsins and the DegV family proteins involved in activation of fatty acids, it was
252    hypothesized that heliorhodopsins might be involved in light-induces membrane lipid modifications
253    (Chazan et al., 2022). However, genes for DegV proteins were not found in the vicinity of heliorhodopsin
254    genes in strains of Geodermatophilaceae (Supplementary Fig. S5), although the DegV genes were present
255    in the majority of members of this family, including the heliorhodopsin-lacking ones. From the above

256    findings it follows that if any relationship between heliorhodopsins and DegV takes place, it is not

258              The heliorhodopsins were also reported to regulate the activity of class 2 cyclobutane pyrimidine
259    dimer photolyase (Shim et al., 2022) and glutamine synthetase (Cho et al., 2022). In both cases, the genes
260    for the target enzymes were adjacent and formed an operon with heliorhodopsins. It has also been noted
261    that the heliorhodopsin genes from some aquatic actinomycetes (actinobacteria in the original work) are
262    surrounded by two large clusters of Nuo genes, the products of which are the key proteins in respiratory
263    chains (Kovalev et al., 2020). A different situation was observed in Geodermatophilaceae, where the
264    heliorhodopsin genes had a diverse vicinity without clear patterns. In addition, they were predominantly
265    oriented in the opposite direction to the nearest neighboring genes. The above data provide clear evidence
266    for the absence of co-expression. Thus, it can be stated that the analysis of neighboring genes does not
267    allow us to make an assumption about functions of heliorhodopsins in the Geodermatophilaceae family.

269              Light-harvesting carotenoid antenna
270              A previous study of Salinibacter ruber (Actinomycetales) has shown that it produces
271    xanthorhodopsin (XR), a well-known light-driven H+ pump (Balashov et al., 2005). In addition to retinal,
272    XR contains a non-covalently bound carotenoid salinixanthin with a function of a light-harvesting antenna
273    (Balashov et al., 2005). In the present study, we have found that a significant number of amino acids
274    involved in carotenoid binding by xanthorhodopsin are present in Type I rhodopsins from
275    Geodermatophilaceae, including glycine at a position homologous to 156 in xanthorhodopsin
276    (Supplementary Fig. S8). Glycine at this position was supposed to be the best diagnostic feature for the
277    possibility of binding the salinixanthin-like carotenoids (Luecke et al., 2008).
278              The Na+-pumping rhodopsin (NaR) from flavobacterium Dokdonia sp. PRO95 was reported to
279    bind carotenoid antenna after replacement of threonine by glycine at position 216 (homolog of G201 for
280    xanthorhodopsin) (Anashkin et al., 2018). Interestingly, glycine at the corresponding position was found in
281    most Type I rhodopsins from Geodermatophilaceae, and only NDQ rhodopsins had methionine instead of
282    glycine. Other carotenoid-binding amino acids identical between xanthorhodopsin and NaR rhodopsin
283    from Dokdonia sp. PRO95 (G156, N191, L197, Y207 in XR numbering) were the same as in the majority
284    of Type I rhodopsins of Geodermatophilaceae. From all the above data it might be supposed that the DTE
285    and NTQ rhodopsins from Geodermatophilaceae may bind the light harvesting antenna and enhance
286    efficiency of ion transport, like that reported for other carotenoid-binding rhodopsins.

312              Pumping specificity
313              Rhodopsins with DTEW and DTEF motifs from G. dictyosporus and G. obscurus
314              GdiWR and GdiFR rhodopsins from G. dictyosporus VKM Ac-659T and GobFR rhodopsin from
315    G. obscurus VKM Ac-658T (Table 1) were found to exhibit outward H+-pumping activity, which depended
316    on the presence of Ca2+ ions and the particular expression strain. In case the rhodopsin genes were
317    expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3), the pumping was observed in both CaCl2 and NaCl solutions, but the
318    activity was higher at CaCl2 (Fig. 3A, 3B, 3G, 3H). On the other hand, Ca 2+ was strictly required for H +-
319    pumping when DTE rhodopsin genes are expressed in strain E. coli C43(DE3) (Fig. 3D, 3E, 3J, 3K).
320    Another divalent ion, namely, Mg2+, showed little effect only in case the strain E. coli BL21(DE3) was
321    used for expression, but no effect was observed with E. coli C43(DE3) expression strain (Fig. 3C, 3F, 3I,
322    3L). The H+-pumping activity was confirmed using cells treated with a protonophore (CCCP) that greatly
323    reduced light-induced pH changes.
324              It is worth mentioning that no Ca 2+ requirement for pumping activities was noted previously for
325    rhodopsins, including those from actinomycetes (Chuon et al., 2021; Dwulit-Smith et al., 2018; Keffer et
326    al., 2015; Nakamura et al., 2016; Pushkarev and Béjà, 2016). However, some papers and guidelines
327    recommend addition of Ca2+ and Mg2+ to pumping solutions at concentrations of 100 μM and 10 mM,
328    respectively (Han et al., 2020; Maresca et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2003). In our experiments, no DTE
329    rhodopsin activity was observed at the above Ca 2+ and Mg2+concentrations when the expression strain E.
330    coli C43(DE3) is used (data not shown). The activity of rhodopsins from other groups studied in this work
331    did not depend on the presence of Ca2+ or on the use a particular E. coli expression strain.
332              Considering the requirement for high Ca2+ concentrations, it is unlikely that it plays a regulatory
333    function. It can be inferred from our study that Ca 2+ in a high concentration is necessary to correct folding
334    of DTE rhodopsins. Although the DTE rhodopsins are colored that indicates the overall right protein
335    folding, the local miss-folds may still prevent rhodopsin pumping activity in the absence of Ca 2+. In
336    addition, Ca2+ can interact with lipid membranes, in particular, it rigidifies and orders lipid bilayers by
337    involving in mechanisms of conformational changes of the lipid headgroup region, ordering of acyl chains
338    and lipid dehydration (Melcrová et al., 2016). Thus, Ca 2+ ions can affect the membrane structure, resulting
339    in the activation of rhodopsins.
340              It is unclear whether Ca2+ is required for pumping process only when the DTE rhodopsins are
341    expressed in Gram-negative (diderm) E. coli, or it also necessary in case the expression strain is Gram-
342    positive (monoderm) prokaryotes like the host Geodermatophilaceae strains. The Gram-positives do not
343    contain outer membrane, possesses different lipid composition and usually has thicker and more rigid cell
344    walls with multilayered peptidoglycans and different layers of heteropolysaccharides and peptides (Rohde,


                                                                                                                                                         12
      bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.05.16.594530; this version posted May 16, 2024. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
                      was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission.




345    2019). On the other hand, the requirement for Ca 2+ can be unique feature of the DTE rhodopsins from
346    Geodermatophilaceae when Ca2+ acts as an activator of these rhodopsins under certain physico-chemical
347    environmental conditions, for instance, in the calcium-rich biotopes. This is consistent with the ability of
348    representatives of the family Geodermatophilaceae to live in such biotopes, for example, in limestone

350    when expressed in E. coli, their functions may somewhat differ in host strains under certain natural
351    conditions. This supposition follows from the co-presence of rhodopsins from both above groups, DTEW
352    and DTEF, in three members of the family Geodermatophilaceae (Supplementary Table S1, S2).

385              Protein sequence analysis revealed also a number of differences in the amino acids between NDQ
386    rhodopsins from Geodermatophilaceae and well-studied NDQ rhodopsins from aquatic Gram-negatives
387    (Supplementary Fig. S10). The most notable differences were observed at positions L74, N61 and G263
388    (in KR2 numbering). The first amino acid residue (L74) plays an important role in switching between Na +-
389    to H+-pumping activities and the last two (N61 and G263) are associated with ion selectivity (Gushchin et
390    al., 2015; Kovalev et al., 2019). In addition, the valine is detected at the position homologous to H180 in
391    KR2. Histidine at this position was reported to be involved in the mechanism of Na +-pumping and is
392    characteristic of all NDQ rhodopsins studied in this respect (Kriebel et al., 2023).
393              It is clear, that the MspDR rhodopsin and most likely other NDQ rhodopsins from
394    Geodermatophilaceae act only as H+ pumps at pH < 6. MspDR also showed slight bifunctional properties
395    at pH 6-7, and may probably act as Na+ pump in some extreme conditions at high pH and salinity. These
396    rhodopsins are apparently incapable of transporting Na+ to a noticeable degree under seawater conditions,
397    but they can do this in case the Na+ content and pH value of the environment are significantly higher than
398    in seawater (>>0.5 M NaCl and pH >>8). This may be important for the survival and vital activity of
399    Geodermatophilaceae species in saline and hyper-arid environments under the salinity level much higher
400    than in seawater (Busarakam et al., 2016; Tarlachkov et al., 2023).
401              On the other hand, members of the family Geodermatophilaceae with NDQ rhodopsins can grow
402    well in the NaCl-lacking nutrient media (for example, ISP2) (Busarakam et al., 2016; Tarlachkov et al.,
403    2023; Xiao et al., 2011), unlike most marine microorganisms with Na +- or Na+/H+-pumping rhodopsins
404    that prefer salt-containing media (Khan et al., 2006; Park et al., 2012; Yoon et al., 2006). It can be
405    therefore supposed that the NDQ rhodopsins revealed in Geodermatophilaceae are capable of performing
406    two functions, namely, to maintain the Na + homeostasis under extreme salinity and the pH balance under
407    acidification of the environment.
408              Previously a pH-sensitive DTS rhodopsin active at pH 6 and below from Gram-negative (diderm)
409    bacterium Sphingomonas paucimobilis promising for use in optogenetics has been reported (Maliar et al.,
410    2020). Microbial rhodopsins with the NDQ motif from actinomycetes of the family Geodermatophilaceae
411    may represent another group of pH-sensitive rhodopsins of interest for optogenetic studies and
412    applications. Further improvement in specificity can be achieved, in particular, by site-directed
413    mutagenesis to completely eliminate the affinity to Na + ions. These proteins can also be used as model to
414    study the mechanism of switching between the H+ and Na+ specificities in NDQ rhodopsins.

442              CONCLUSION
443              New data have been provided on the diversity of rhodopsins in actinomycetes of the family
444    Geodermatophilaceae (class Actinomycetes, phylum Actinomycetota) mostly originating from hot and arid
445    ecosystems, and some new properties and functional characteristics of the rhodopsins have been reported.
446    Rhodopsins from all five groups studied in the present work, both the newly discovered NTQ and
447    heliorhodopsins, and the DTEW, DTEF and NDQ rhodopsins previously identified in the
448    Geodermatophilaceae, have either unique absorbance maxima or unique features of pumping activity. The
449    retinal proteins of Gram-positive (diderm) Geodermatophilaceae share quite low similarities to the
450    respective rhodopsins from Gram-negatives (monoderms) inhabiting marine and freshwater environments
451    and also differ from them in some highly conserved amino acid residues. This is not surprising given the
452    evolutionary distances and phenotypic differences between rhodopsin-producing members of
453    Actinomycetota and representatives of the other bacterial phyla, as well as remarkable variations between
454    their specialized biotopes, both aquatic and terrestrial, including rocks and desert sandy soils. Further
455    study will extend our knowledge about the diversity and functional characteristics of the
456    Geodermatophilaceae rhodopsins and their role in adaptation of actinomycetes of this family to harsh
457    environments.
